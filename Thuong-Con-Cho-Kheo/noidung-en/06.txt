CHUONG: Exam season and choosing a path
EPI: Your child needs someone beside the track handing out water, not one more person holding a stopwatch.
LEAD: In exam season the whole household seems to hold its breath. Parents tiptoe, cook nourishing food, and worry. That worry is love, but what the child receives is weight. Then come the choices of school and career, where a child's dream and a parent's experience can collide head-on. This chapter is about walking with your child through those bends without taking the wheel.

## The night before the exam your child cannot sleep
BC: Eleven at night before the graduation exam. Mai is still flipping through her notes, hands ice cold. She tells Dad: I've forgotten everything, I can't remember a thing.
PX: Nothing to worry about. You're a good student, you'll pass for sure. I believe you'll get a high score. | Dad expects a high score. If I can't do it, he will collapse. One more thing to fear.
BA: (Sits down beside her, takes her hand.) So cold. Are you shaking?
CON: I'm so scared, Dad. Whatever page I open, it feels like I never studied it.
BA: I know that feeling. It was the same for me; the night before, the words seemed to dance on the page. You haven't forgotten. You're frightened, and when we're frightened the mind locks.
CON: What if it's the same in the exam room tomorrow?
BA: Then do exactly what I'm showing you now: put down the pen, take four slow breaths, read the easiest question first. Do one question and it unlocks. Now close the notes; nothing more will go in tonight.
CON: Aren't you afraid I'll fail?
BA: I am only afraid you'll be worn out. Tomorrow, whatever you manage is yours. Whatever the result, tomorrow afternoon I'm still taking you for beef noodle soup as promised.
CON: ...Sit with me a little longer.
BA: Yes. I'll sit until you're sleepy.
VS: A little nervousness keeps us alert, but too much temporarily freezes memory: the knowledge is still there but cannot be retrieved. You're good, you'll pass does not reduce the worry, because it denies the fear and piles on expectation. What lowers anxiety is having someone accept it as normal, slower breathing, and an assurance that love does not depend on the result.
BH: The night before an exam, your child needs no more knowledge and no more expectations. They need sleep, and to know you can welcome any result.
LN: If you have a child facing exams, promise tonight one specific thing you will do afterwards whatever the result: a meal, a short outing. And practise together the four slow breaths for when the mind locks.
CN: Whatever you manage tomorrow is yours. In the afternoon I'm still taking you for noodles.
TN: Are my encouraging words secretly carrying expectations?
TN: During my child's exam season, who at home is the most anxious, and in what form does that anxiety reach my child?

## Your child wants a field you do not approve of
BC: Dang, in grade 12, is solid in every subject and his parents have Economics in mind. At dinner he says: I want to apply for Graphic Design. Dad puts down his chopsticks.
PX: Drawing doesn't pay. We didn't raise you to draw adverts. Study Economics; when you graduate I'll get you a job. | Dad doesn't take what I love seriously. It's my life and he decides. If anything goes wrong later, it's on him.
BA: (Quiet for a moment.) Honestly, I'm surprised, and I'm worried. But I want to hear first. When did you start liking that field?
CON: Since grade 10. I taught myself online, I made posters for the club, a logo for a friend's sister's shop. People have already paid me, Dad.
BA: You have paying clients and I didn't know. May I see some of what you've made?
CON: (Opens the laptop.) Here. This one took three weeks.
BA: ...I'm no expert, but it looks like real work. Now let me tell you my worries and you tell me what you think: I'm afraid this field is very competitive, the income unstable, and machines will take it over. What do you make of that?
CON: I've looked into it. It is unstable if you can only draw. So I plan to learn app interface design as well; that area needs people.
BA: You've researched more than I expected. Then here is my suggestion: this summer you intern for a month at a design company; I'll find a place. See what the real job is like. If you still want it afterwards, I'll back you. Deal?
CON: Deal! But you mean it, right?
BA: I mean it. It's your life and you hold the wheel. I'll sit beside you and help read the map.
VS: Parents usually object out of love: they want to steer the child away from a road that looks risky. But a child forced into a field carries two burdens, studying something unwanted and losing the sense that life is their own. When difficulty comes they let go easily, because it was never my choice. A child who is heard, seriously questioned and allowed to try the real job usually chooses more maturely, even if in the end they change their own mind.
BH: You may voice your worries and ask hard questions. But the wheel of your child's life must be in their hands, because they are the one who will travel that whole road.
LN: Ask three questions and only listen, no rebuttal: What do you do that makes you lose track of time? How do you picture a working day? Which job would you most like to try if you could watch it for a week?
CN: I am worried, and I'm telling you so you know. But it's your life and you hold the wheel.
TN: Do I want that field because my child suits it, or because it calms me?
TN: Was there a dream of mine that was blocked? How do I feel about that now?

## Your child studies day and night and dares not rest
BC: Linh, in grade 12, is top of her class. Lately she eats with a book in hand, the light is on at two in the morning, she has lost four kilos and her hair is falling out. She cries over a 9.5 because of the missing half mark. When Mum suggests a rest she snaps: you don't understand.
PX: I'm glad to see you studying like this. Just a few more months, darling; once you're in you can rest all you like. | My worth is my results. My health ranks below my marks, and Mum thinks so too.
ME: (Knocks at eleven with some fruit.) May I sit for five minutes? I won't stop you studying.
CON: I still have three papers to do, Mum.
ME: I know. I'll say one thing and go. Yesterday I saw you cry over a 9.5. I lay awake wondering: has anyone in this house ever told you that you need a ten to be loved?
CON: Nobody said it... But if I slip, everyone will be disappointed. My teacher, you and Dad, everyone thinks I must get into a top university.
ME: So you are carrying a whole crowd's expectations. That is too heavy. Let me take mine back: I do not need you to get into a top university. I need you to be able to eat, sleep, and still laugh.
CON: (Tearing up.) I'm so scared, Mum. When I stop, I feel like I'm losing.
ME: I understand. But running scared without resting, you'll have no fuel left on exam day. Let's try one week: lights out at midnight, Sunday afternoons off. If your marks drop, I take responsibility. Just one week, with me.
VS: A perfectionist child looks like every parent's dream, but inside there is often fear: the fear of having worth only when on top. These children do not enjoy high marks; they only escape, briefly, the fear of low ones. Long-term sleep loss and exhaustion reduce learning efficiency, which leads to still more study, a spiral. Weight loss, hair loss and tears over half a mark are the body speaking up on the child's behalf.
BH: A child who overworks does not need to be praised for diligence. They need permission to rest, and assurance that they have worth even when they are not first.
LN: Tell your child one clear sentence: We do not love you for your marks. Then set together a fixed lights-out time and one full rest period each week. Keep those hours yourself so your child does not feel they are resting alone.
CN: I don't need you to come first. I need you to eat, to sleep, and still to laugh.
TN: When my child gets a high mark my joy shows. When the mark is ordinary, what does my face do? What does my child read there?
TN: Can my child rest without feeling guilty?

## Your child does not know what they like
BC: Three months until applications. Tam, in grade 12, is an average-to-good student who neither hates nor loves any subject. When Mum asks what field he wants, he shrugs: I don't know. You pick.
PX: Eighteen years old and you don't know what you like. Your friends all have a direction. You have no goals at all! | Not knowing what I like is a fault. I am behind my friends at the very core.
ME: You say you don't know, and I believe you really don't. Not knowing at eighteen is very common; I was the same.
CON: You were? But you're an accountant.
ME: I picked it at random because my friend applied for it. Luckily it turned out to suit me. But I don't want you to pick at random as I did. Let's try another route: you don't need to know what you like, only what you can't stand.
CON: I can't stand sitting in one place for eight hours. And I hate having to talk to strangers all day.
ME: Good, two kinds of work ruled out. And when does time pass fastest for you?
CON: When I fix motorbikes with Uncle Ut. And when I build computers for my friends.
ME: So your hands like to work; you like making broken things run. Several paths go that way, university and vocational. This Sunday let's invite Uncle Ut for coffee and you ask him about his trade.
CON: Would you find it strange if I went to vocational school?
ME: I find that people doing work that suits them live happily. Strange would be four years studying something you don't care for.
VS: Society demands that eighteen-year-olds have a clear passion, while most adults find their direction by trying things, not by sitting and thinking. A child who says I don't know is not empty; they simply have too few experiences to compare. Ruling out what they cannot stand and following the moments when time disappears is far more practical than asking what is your passion. And a less glamorous path that fits the hands often lasts longer than a degree left unused.
BH: Not knowing what you like at eighteen is no fault. Passion seldom arrives by itself; it grows out of chances to try.
LN: With your child, list two columns: things I do and lose track of time, and things I cannot stand. Then find a real person working in something close to the first column for your child to talk to for thirty minutes.
CN: Not knowing yet what you like is normal. Let's go and try things together.
TN: Do I know what my child is doing when they lose track of time?
TN: Am I ranking jobs as respectable and lowly in my head? Can my child hear it?
