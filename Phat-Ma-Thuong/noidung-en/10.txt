CHUONG: Skipping class, truancy, present in body only
EPI: A student who skips school is running from something. Your job is to find out what, not to haul him back.
LEAD: Skipping a lesson, missing every Monday, sitting in class as if elsewhere: three different ways of giving up. Punishing by counting sessions pushes the student further away. This chapter offers ways to keep students by understanding why they leave, with a few light moves to give them a reason to return.

## A student skips one lesson to go out
LOI: The school reports Kien skipped third period, slipping out after break to the drink stall at the end of the lane with two friends. Back in the next lesson he says he had a stomach ache. You know he is lying.
CHIEU: The lesson taught back | Do not interrogate. Say: in third period I taught something fairly important and you missed it. Learn it from a friend and teach it back to me in five minutes, like a teacher. If he understood, add a point; if not, coach him further. Also ask: what about that lesson or the class made you want to be outside? If he can say, listen.
THAY: Kien, I know you did not have a stomach ache, and I am not angry. In the lesson you missed I taught something important; ask a friend to tell you and tomorrow teach it back to me for five minutes.
TRO: Um... I am sorry, sir. I found that lesson boring.
THAY: Thank you for being honest. Tell me where it felt boring, I want to know.
VS: Punishing skipping by prohibition only makes him find better hiding places. Teaching it back makes him absorb the missed lesson and turns it into a light debt. Asking the reason opens information that helps you adjust.
RG: Do not use it with a student who skips from fear of bullying or trouble at home. Do not make him teach before the class if he is shy. If he skips repeatedly, tell parents in a friendly way.
BT: Let him teach it back to you privately at your desk instead of before the class.
CN: In the lesson you missed I taught something important. Learn it from a friend and teach me for five minutes.

## A student skips the subject he fears
LOI: Thy always skips PE, or escapes Maths when there is oral testing. She often asks to go to the toilet and does not come back. She is not a bad student.
CHIEU: A back door with a reason | Meet Thy privately and say: I notice there are lessons you often do not want to enter. I will not ask the reason. You just choose: I give you a small legitimate reason to stay out or arrive late during oral tests, as an agreement, and in return you tell me beforehand. I also promise to test you orally in a way you are comfortable with, such as in writing or privately after the lesson.
THAY: Thy, I see there are lessons you often want to step out of. I will not ask why. You tell me beforehand and we agree on another way, so you still learn without leaving.
TRO: Um... I am afraid of being called to the board, sir.
THAY: Thank you for telling me. I promise not to call you up suddenly; I will tell you a day ahead.
VS: She skips from fear of being judged or embarrassed, not laziness. The agreement to tell you beforehand turns skipping into negotiation, and the gentler testing reduces her fear. She sees you trust her.
RG: Do not use it as a lasting entitlement if the fear grows. If she is badly anxious about coming to class, suggest the family take her to a school counsellor.
BT: Give the oral question a day ahead so she can prepare and feel confident.
CN: Tell me before you feel afraid and we will agree another way.

## A student is absent every Monday
LOI: For three weeks in a row Giang has missed Monday, with vague excuses. She still seems flustered when you ask. You suspect something but do not know what.
CHIEU: The Sunday call | Phone Giang on Sunday evening and say exactly three things: I called to ask how you are, I am not asking about your absence, I hope to see you tomorrow. If she does not pick up, leave a short message. No investigation, no blame. When she returns, say only: I am glad to see you. After a few weeks she tells the reason herself.
THAY: Giang, I just called to ask how you are. I am not asking about absences. I hope to see you in class tomorrow.
TRO: Um... thank you, sir. I will come tomorrow.
THAY: Thank you. If there is anything you need, just message me.
VS: A pattern of absence often carries a reason she is not yet ready to say. A check-in call with no blame shows her someone remembers her and does not judge. The feeling of belonging often draws a student back more than any reminder.
RG: Do not call repeatedly. If you suspect abuse or that she has to work, consult the school and its support staff. Do not press her to tell.
BT: Send a note via the class monitor about that day's lesson, with the words: I kept this for you.
CN: I just called to ask how you are. I hope to see you in class tomorrow.

## A small group skips class together
LOI: Hung, Tai and Son skipped the last lesson of the afternoon to play games. All three were found out and are very anxious, but still smile awkwardly as if nothing happened.
CHIEU: Three guest advisers | Invite the three to a short session you call the Risk Management Sub-committee. Say: you three have just experienced an event and I need your help knowing how to keep other students from skipping. Each writes three ways to stop friends skipping without using punishment. The three think like rule-makers, and often they themselves see why they were wrong.
THAY: The three of you see me for a moment. I need your help: you are experts who have just skipped class, so write three ways to stop others skipping, without using punishment.
TRO: Um... experts, sir? We can do that.
THAY: I trust you. After I read them I will choose one to bring to the class, with your names on it.
VS: Turning offenders into advisers moves them from defence to cooperation. Having to think of ways to prevent skipping puts them in the position of the enforcer, so they look back at their own act. Rules they helped shape are also easier to follow.
RG: Use only for mild, first-time faults. Do not make the list of the three public. Still inform parents if regulations require.
BT: Pick one of their proposals and trial it for a week.
CN: You three are experts who have just skipped class; help me think of three ways to keep others from skipping.

## A student wants to quit school altogether
LOI: Minh, in grade 11, tells you: I plan to leave school and go to work. He says it calmly, as if decided. His marks are low and he feels he cannot keep up. You are worried.
CHIEU: The classroom still has a seat | Do not lecture. Say: I understand you are carrying a lot. I ask one thing of you: do not decide this week. We meet every day at break for five days to talk; you tell me, and I just listen. Also invite family and school to look for solutions, such as vocational training or an adjusted timetable. His seat in the class stays as it is.
THAY: Minh, I hear you. I ask only one thing: do not decide this week. Every day at break we talk for five minutes, and I only listen.
TRO: Um... let me think a bit more, sir.
THAY: Your seat in the class is still there. You think, I will wait.
VS: The decision to quit often follows a string of feeling like a failure. Extending the time to decide, letting him be heard, and opening other options shows him there are still roads. Keeping his seat is a concrete invitation to return.
RG: Do not lecture or threaten about the future. Do not promise what you cannot deliver. You must coordinate with family and school, especially if he shows signs of depression or is being forced to leave.
BT: Introduce him to a career counsellor or an older student who has succeeded in vocational training.
CN: I hear you. Do not decide this week; let us talk five minutes every day.
