CHUONG: Group work: freeloading, taking over, taking sides
EPI: Group work is where students learn to live with one another. Punishment within a group must make the group better, not more afraid.
LEAD: Every time group work is set, teachers hear the same complaints: he does nothing, she does it all, those two are not speaking. The common thread is that students must learn to cooperate, and experience teaches that better than lectures. This chapter offers moves for each type of group fault, each with a light penalty and real learning.

## One student freeloads in the group
LOI: A group of six: four work, one stands and watches, one uses a phone. The whole group is annoyed but dares not say. At the end the product has everyone's names on it, though two did nothing.
CHIEU: My part log | Each member has a small slip on which, during forty minutes, they note: what I have done, what I will do next. At the end you go round and gently read each slip. You punish nobody, you simply read the parts that exist for the group to hear. If a student's slip is blank, you ask very lightly: what is your part, so the group can arrange it? At that point he often speaks up and takes a task instead of sitting stubbornly.
THAY: Each of you, note down your own part in this slip during the session. At the end I will read out to the group the parts that are there.
TRO: Um... I have not done much yet, sir.
THAY: That is fine. Pick one small part to finish in the last ten minutes; the group will wait for you.
VS: Freeloading is often because the student does not know what to do, or fears getting it wrong. A slip listing each part makes the boundaries of responsibility clear without anyone blaming anyone. When his job appears on paper, he feels a light but clear pressure to act.
RG: Do not read an empty slip aloud before the class. Do not punish the whole group for one person. If he does not work because he does not understand the material, help him first.
BT: Let the group assign jobs with coloured cards, each student drawing one, so everyone has a task.
CN: Each of you note your own part, and at the end I will look at it with the whole group.

## One student takes over and will not let anyone join in
LOI: Hanh is a strong student who always does the whole group task herself for fear the others will do it poorly. The group is annoyed at not contributing, and she is tired from carrying everyone. The product is beautiful but the group has learned nothing.
CHIEU: The team leader's five minutes of silence | Tell Hanh privately: I set you a harder task. For the next five minutes you sit still and only observe, no pen in hand, while the others work. At the end of five minutes she tells you what she saw. After that she may only act as guide: asking, prompting, suggesting, never doing it for them. She sees that others' errors are chances to learn, and in the end the others understand the lesson.
THAY: Hanh, I am setting you something harder than doing the work: for the next five minutes you may not touch a pen, you only observe while the others do it. Afterwards tell me what you saw.
TRO: Um... that is hard, sir. I saw they were slow.
THAY: Slow is also a way of learning. You guide only, do not do it for them. I believe you can.
VS: She takes over from anxiety, not selfishness. Making her learn to let go shows that group work is not doing everything yourself. The others get a chance to speak, and the product belongs to the group.
RG: Do not call her domineering. Do not assign a task that dampens her initiative. Praise her responsibility before the challenge.
BT: Give Hanh the role of secretary recording the others' contributions, sitting beside them without doing it for them.
CN: Help me try something hard: for the next five minutes you only observe, and let the others do it.

## Two students are angry with each other and will not work together
LOI: Khue and Ngan used to be close friends. Now they are cross and not speaking. They are in the same group but sit at opposite ends, doing two separate parts that do not fit. The group is at a loss.
CHIEU: The shared pen | Hand the two one pen and one sheet of paper: the two of you hold this one pen and write a short passage describing what you both like about the group. Time ten minutes. Neither may let go of the pen; each writes just one word and passes it on. They laugh and exchange ideas. You do not mention the quarrel; you simply let the game do its work.
THAY: You two share this one pen. You write one word, hand it over, your friend writes the next, and so on, ten minutes to write a passage about what you like in your group.
TRO: (Khue and Ngan, hesitantly.) Um... we start now, sir?
THAY: Start. I do not grade it. I just want to see what you two write.
VS: Quarrels need a shared task to open the door without anyone having to say it outright. The shared pen forces cooperation, and the fun of the game eases the tension. Reconciliation happens as a side effect of doing something.
RG: Do not force reconciliation. Do not speak about the quarrel before a crowd. If the conflict is serious, arrange to talk privately.
BT: Have the two make a small craft item for the class together, such as a card, each doing half.
CN: You two share one pen, one word each, and write me a passage.

## A group hands in sloppy work just to finish
LOI: Group Four's product is obviously rushed: content thin, presentation careless. The group says they did their best. You know they are capable of far more.
CHIEU: A draft exhibition | Do not grade it at once. Pin the group's draft on the board with a slip listing two things they themselves think good and two things they want to upgrade. Invite other groups to walk round, each leaving a kindly suggestion slip. The group has two days to upgrade; marks count the final version. They see their work through others' eyes and raise their own standard.
THAY: I am not grading this yet. The group lists two good things and two things to upgrade, and I pin it up for other groups to comment. Two days from now we hand in the final version.
TRO: Um... our group can redo it, sir.
THAY: I believe you can do better. This draft is just a first version.
VS: Sloppy work usually comes from nobody looking at the product with outside eyes. A draft exhibition creates viewers and the motive to raise their own standard. Marks counted only on the final version give the group opportunity instead of pressure.
RG: Comments may be about the product only, not the people. Do not let groups compare and mock. If the group is weak from lacking skills, give extra support.
BT: Invite a student from another group to play customer and ask questions about the product.
CN: This is the first version; in two days we hand in the final one. List two things you want to upgrade.

## The class splits into rival camps
LOI: Two opposing cliques have emerged in the class; they do not speak at break, and in group work they refuse to be put together. One student is isolated for belonging to the other side.
CHIEU: Change groups by lots | Announce that this week every group task will be redrawn by lots. Set a fun rule: each new group has one small mandatory task of finding three things its members have in common, none already known. At the end of the lesson each group reads its three. Gradually the lines between camps blur, as students discover the one on the other side also likes football or also fears Physics.
THAY: This week we draw lots for groups. Each group has a small task: find three things the members have in common, things nobody knew yet. At the end of the lesson we listen.
TRO: Um... we really draw lots, sir?
THAY: Really. I believe our class has more in common than we think.
VS: Camps arise from little contact. Random reshuffling plus a task of finding commonalities creates purposeful contact, the most effective way to reduce prejudice. You do not have to mention the conflict openly.
RG: Do not shuffle so as to deliberately seat two quarrelling students together without preparation. If one student is badly isolated, watch separately and talk to those involved.
BT: Make a class chart of commonalities; whoever finds the best of the week gets written on it.
CN: This week we draw lots for groups; each group finds three things in common that nobody yet knows.
