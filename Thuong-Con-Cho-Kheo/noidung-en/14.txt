CHUONG: Brothers, sisters and what is fair
EPI: Children do not need equal shares. They need to be loved individually.
LEAD: All parents say they love their children the same, and every child at some point is convinced they are loved less. Siblings are the first people with whom a child must share the most precious thing in their life: their parents. So a fight over a toy, who washes the dishes, a new baby: none of these is ever only about that. This chapter is about parents giving up the judge's seat, and about making sure each child has a place nobody can take.

## Two children fighting, each saying the other started it
BC: Screams from the living room. Six-year-old Su is wailing, nine-year-old Bin is red in the face, and the TV remote is on the floor. Mum runs in: Bin says she scratched me first, Su says he grabbed it from me.
PX: Bin! You're older, you should give way! A big boy picking on his little sister. Apologise now! | Bin hears: Mum always takes her side; being older is a crime. Su hears: cry loudly and you win.
ME: (Picks up the remote, steps between them.) I can see you are both very angry. Is anyone hurt? Bin, you have a scratch on your hand. Su, your shoulder is red.
CON: (Bin.) She scratched me first! I was watching and she jumped in and grabbed it.
ME: I wasn't here, so I don't know who was first, and I'm not ruling on who was first. I only know this: hitting and scratching are not allowed, for either of you. Now each of you sits on a chair and breathes until the anger is out. I'll hold the remote.
ME: (Five minutes later.) Now each of you says one sentence: what I want. Nothing about the other one. Bin first.
CON: (Bin.) I want to finish my episode. Ten more minutes.
ME: Su, what do you want?
CON: (Su.) I want to watch cartoons. He's always watching.
ME: So there is one TV and two people who want two things. This is your problem to solve, not mine. Talk to each other about how to share. When you have a way you both accept, come and get the remote. I'll be in the kitchen.
CON: (Bin, three minutes later.) We've decided. I watch my ten minutes, then Su watches two episodes, and tomorrow Su chooses first.
ME: Su, do you agree? You do. Then here's the remote. I like how you two worked that out yourselves.
VS: When parents jump in to rule on right and wrong, every quarrel becomes a trial whose prize is the parent's backing. The loser resents; the winner learns to play the victim. The older one must give way is especially harmful: it teaches the older child that age is an offence and the younger that weakness is a right. A more useful role for parents is safety keeper and facilitator: stop violence, let each child state their need, and hand the problem back to them.
BH: Do not be the judge of who started it. Stop the fighting, hear what each one wants, and hand the problem back to the two of them.
LN: Next time your children argue without violence, try not stepping in for three minutes. If you must, say only: I see you two have a problem. I trust you can find a way. Call me if you need help.
CN: I'm not ruling on who started it. Talk to each other, and tell me when you have a way you both accept.
TN: Which child do I side with by reflex? Does the other one know?
TN: As a child, was I the older one who had to give way, or the younger one who was defended? What did that leave in me?

## Your child says you love the baby more
BC: There is a new three-month-old baby. Seven-year-old Khoi, always well behaved, has started wetting the bed again and whining, and once pinched the baby. That evening he says, dry-eyed: you only love the baby. I'm the spare one.
PX: Nonsense! We love you both the same. You're big now; you should love your little brother, not be jealous of him. | I really am sad and Mum says it's nonsense. Jealousy is bad. I am both the spare one and a nasty child.
ME: (Hands the baby to Dad, pulls Khoi onto her lap.) You feel like the spare one. That is such a sad thing to feel. Tell me, since the baby came, what has changed for you?
CON: You don't read me stories any more. You're always holding the baby. When I call you, you say shh, wait a bit. And then you forget.
ME: ...All of that is true. For three months I have shushed you a lot. Not because I love the baby more, but because he is tiny and can't do anything alone, so he takes nearly all my time. But you have truly lost me, I understand.
CON: It was more fun before the baby.
ME: Yes, back then you had all of me. It's all right to miss that; you're not bad for thinking it. Here is what I'll do: every evening at eight, Dad takes the baby and I am Khoi's alone for twenty minutes. Stories, or whatever you like. Even if the baby cries, Dad will handle it; I won't run out.
CON: Really? Every evening?
ME: Every evening. We'll call it Khoi time. I'll write it on the calendar.
CON: Then tonight we read the dinosaur book.
ME: The dinosaur book. And let me tell you a secret: when you were his size, I held you all day just like this. Let me get the photos to show you.
VS: For a child, a new sibling means losing the only-one place in their parents' hearts, a loss as great as an adult being replaced. Regressions like bed-wetting, whining or baby talk are the child's way of saying I need looking after too. Denying the feeling with we love you the same convinces nobody, because the child is counting in minutes, and counting correctly. What heals is admitting the truth, allowing mixed feelings about the baby, and setting aside regular, protected time that nothing interrupts.
BH: Do not say we love you both the same. Give each child a time that belongs to them alone with a parent, regularly, with nobody else in it.
LN: Give each child fifteen to twenty minutes of their own with Mum or Dad, with a name and a fixed time, written on the calendar. In that time the child chooses the activity, and your phone is in another room.
CN: It's all right to miss the time before the baby. Tonight at eight, I am yours alone.
TN: How many minutes did I spend alone with each child this past week?
TN: When my child is jealous of a sibling, do I hear naughty or missing me?

## Your child says you are unfair
BC: Hanh, in grade 10, must be home by ten. Her older brother, a first-year student, may stay out until midnight. The youngest, in grade 3, never washes dishes. At dinner Hanh bursts out: everything in this house is unfair to me! He's allowed everything and the little one does nothing!
PX: No keeping score! Your brother is a boy and grown up. Your sister is small. You are a girl, in the middle, and should know better. | I lose out because I am a girl and in the middle, two things I did not choose. If I say so, I am keeping score.
BA: Hanh says unfair. I want to hear it clearly, one thing at a time. Curfew first.
CON: He can be out until midnight and I have to be back at ten. And when he was in grade 10 he was allowed until eleven; Mum said so.
BA: (Looks at Mum. She nods.) ...So at the same age you've been held an hour tighter than he was. I never noticed. I think the reason is that I worry more about a daughter. The worry is real, but for you it has become unfairness; I see that.
CON: I don't go anywhere bad. I go to club meetings and come straight home.
BA: I know. So we fix this: your curfew matches his in grade 10, eleven on Saturday nights, with exactly the conditions I set for him: tell us where, answer your phone, come home with someone. Now, the dishes?
CON: The little one does nothing. I always wash up.
BA: At eight she can't wash dishes properly yet, but she can do an eight-year-old's jobs: clear the table, wipe it, hang the towels. From tomorrow we re-divide. We don't divide equally in this house, because the three of you are three different ages. But everyone has a share, according to their strength. Is that all right, Hanh?
CON: Yes. ...I thought you'd tell me off for keeping score.
BA: When you're right, I listen. Next time something seems lopsided, say so; don't let it build until it explodes.
VS: Children have an extremely sensitive scale for fairness, and they are right more often than parents like to admit. Fair does not mean identical: children of different ages have different rights and duties. But the reasons must be explainable, and based on age and responsibility, not on sex or birth order. Middle children and daughters often carry the short end without anyone noticing. Hearing a child's complaint and correcting course when they are right teaches something big: speaking up properly gets results.
BH: Fair is not equal shares. Fair is each child treated according to their age and strength, with parents able to explain why.
LN: Write out each child's privileges and chores side by side. For every difference ask: is the reason age and ability, or because she is a girl, he is the eldest, this one is easiest to order about? Whatever you cannot explain, change.
CN: If something seems lopsided, say so. If you're right, I'll fix it.
TN: Where are sons and daughters in our home treated differently in ways I have never named?
TN: Which child asks for the least? Is it because they have enough, or because they have stopped hoping?

## The good one and the difficult one
BC: Two sisters. Thao, in grade 8, is clever, tidy, praised by every relative. Tran, in grade 6, is an average student, forgetful, hot-tempered. At home the line is often heard: why can't you be like your sister? Lately Tran is more and more stubborn and says: I'm the bad one in this family anyway.
PX: Nobody has to remind your sister and she manages herself. You never listen however often you're told. Learn something from her! | The good-child role is taken. Only the bad-child role is left for me. So I'll play it properly.
ME: (Driving Tran to the shops, just the two of them.) Yesterday you said you're the bad one in the family. I lost sleep over that. Do you really think so?
CON: Well, it's true. Thao is good at everything. I'm worse at everything.
ME: I think I'm the one who made you believe it, because I keep bringing her up. I'm sorry. Comparing like that is wrong, and from now on I stop.
CON: You say that, and when we get home you'll say it again.
ME: I might, out of habit. So I'm asking you: whenever I slip and say why can't you be like your sister, raise your hand and I will stop. I mean it.
CON: (Doubtful.) ...I raise my hand and you stop?
ME: I stop. And let me tell you something I've noticed for a long time and never said. The day Grandma was ill, who sat rubbing her legs all afternoon? When the cat got stuck, who climbed up to free it? Which person is the only one the little boy next door will play with? You have something Thao doesn't: you see what someone needs and you jump right in.
CON: None of that gets marks.
ME: No marks, but out in the world it is badly needed. Thao is good in her way. You are good in yours. I don't need two Thaos. I need one Thao and one Tran.
CON: (Turns to the window; after a while.) Buy me a milk tea.
ME: All right. And one for your sister; you choose her flavour.
VS: In families, roles are handed out unconsciously: the clever one, the good one, the stubborn one, the funny one. Once one child holds the good-child role, the other finds it hard to compete and takes another role in order to be seen, even a bad one, because being scolded still beats being overlooked. Every comparison stamps that role deeper. What undoes it is ending comparison and actively pointing out the specific, evidenced strengths of the child carrying the bad label. The child with the good label needs freeing too: they carry the pressure of never being allowed to fail.
BH: Each child needs a place of their own that they need not fight a sibling for. You do not need two alike. You need each to be themselves.
LN: Write three strengths for each child, the kind that never appear on a report card. This week tell each child one of them, privately, with a specific incident as evidence.
CN: I don't need you to be like your sister. I need one of her and one of you.
TN: What label does each child in our home carry? Who put it there?
TN: Is my good child afraid of disappointing us?
