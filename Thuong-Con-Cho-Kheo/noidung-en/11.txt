CHUONG: The phone that sits between you and your child
EPI: Snatching the phone from a child's hand is easy. The hard part is putting something more worthwhile into the empty hand.
LEAD: Nothing causes more arguments at home than the screen. Parents see a child being swallowed. The child sees parents who understand nothing about their world. And both sides hold a phone more than they think. This chapter does not treat screens as the enemy. It discusses rules a child will actually keep, how to tell keen play from dependence, and the hardest part: looking at ourselves.

## Told to switch off, your child explodes
BC: Mum says it a third time: game off, dinner. Bao, in grade 6, does not look up: wait, I'm in the middle of a match! Mum walks over and pulls the plug. He yells, throws the controller and kicks the chair.
PX: So you dare shout at me? Rude to your mother over a game! I'll smash this machine! | Mum doesn't care what I'm doing. She breaks things when she wants. I hate her, and I'll play in secret.
ME: (Next day, when he is not playing.) Bao, about yesterday. Pulling the plug mid-match was me losing my temper; I own that. And you throwing things and kicking the chair is not acceptable either. Neither of us did well.
CON: You pulled it when I was in a ranked match. If I lose, I lose points, and I made the whole team lose.
ME: Oh, there's a team? I didn't know. I thought games could be paused any time.
CON: No. A match is twenty minutes and once you're in you can't leave. If you leave you get penalised.
ME: So all this time, when I said switch off right now, I was asking for something you couldn't do. And you never explained; you just said wait. Let's redo this. Dinner here is at six thirty. So your last match has to start before when?
CON: Before six. If I'm not in a match by five past six, I won't start one.
ME: Reasonable. I'll give notice: at ten to six I'll say ten minutes left. After that, no new match. And if you start one anyway?
CON: Then no playing for a day.
ME: Agreed. And when you have time, show me a match. I want to know what makes it so gripping.
VS: Games and videos are designed without natural stopping points, and being cut off mid-way is genuinely upsetting, like having the television switched off at the climax of a film. A child's brain cannot yet brake against those designs, so it needs an outside brake. But the brake that works is one announced in advance and set together. When parents understand how the game works, the house rule fits reality and the child sees it as fair.
BH: Screen rules must be made when both are calm, with warning before time is up, and in line with how the game works. Pulling the plug mid-match wins only one evening.
LN: Watch your child play their favourite game for fifteen minutes and ask like a learner: what's the goal, how long is a round, which role are you best at? Only then discuss rules. Children listen to someone who understands their game.
CN: Show me a match. I want to understand, and then we'll set rules that make sense.
TN: Do I know the names of the games, channels and personalities my child is into?
TN: Who sets the screen rules at home, and does my child have any say?

## Your child is on the phone until two in the morning
BC: At two in the morning Mum gets up for water and sees blue light under her grade 9 daughter's door. For weeks Thy has struggled to get up, with dark circles and falling marks. Mum opens the door; Thy shoves the phone under her pillow.
PX: No wonder! Hand it over, confiscated! No phone at all until your marks go up! | Mum spies on me. Without a phone I lose all my friends. I'll find another way, borrow a friend's old one.
ME: (Next morning, Sunday.) I saw you awake until two last night. I'm not taking the phone. I want to understand: what pulls you in so much at that hour?
CON: I just watch clips. I watch one and another comes up, and I mean to watch one more and sleep...
ME: And then it's two hours. It happens to me too; the other day I scrolled until one and nearly fainted in the morning. It isn't you being weak. Those apps have thousands of engineers working out how to stop us letting go.
CON: I know, and I still can't stop. Every morning I regret it.
ME: That you see it yourself is precious. So let's not pit willpower against it; we'd lose. Let's put it further away. My proposal: at ten at night, the whole family's phones charge in the living room. Mine, Dad's, yours. No exceptions.
CON: You two will leave yours out too?
ME: Yes. I need sleep as well. If you need an alarm I'll buy a clock.
CON: What about the group chat? My friends text late. In the morning I'll be a dinosaur.
ME: Tell them: my mum collects phones at ten. Blame it all on me. Maybe someone else is hoping for an excuse to go to sleep.
VS: Sleep loss is the clearest and best-proven harm phones do to teenagers. They need eight to ten hours a night, and late-night light and stimulation push sleep back. Lack of sleep brings irritability, poor concentration and low mood, easily mistaken for bad behaviour or laziness. Confiscation cuts off a child's social life and invites secrecy. A rule about where devices spend the night, applied to the whole family, addresses the most harmful part while remaining acceptable to the child.
BH: Do not ask your child to beat a device built so that nobody can. Let it sleep in the living room, next to yours.
LN: Buy a shared charging station for the living room and an alarm clock for each bedroom. Tonight you are the first to plug in your phone at ten.
CN: It isn't that you're weak. Everybody loses to that thing. So we will all leave ours in the living room.
TN: Where will my phone sleep tonight?
TN: How many hours did my child sleep each night this past week?

## Parents are glued to their screens too
BC: Dinner. Dad eats while answering work messages. Mum scrolls a live sale. Na, in grade 4, tells a story from class; by the second attempt nobody has responded. She takes out the tablet. Dad looks up: no screens at dinner, put it away!
PX: We look at phones because of work. Children don't; put it away now! | There are two sets of rules in this house. My story is less interesting than my parents' phones.
CON: Why can you look and I can't?
BA: (About to snap, then stops. Puts the phone down, face down.) ...You're right. I told you to put it away while holding mine all this time.
CON: I told the story about Mit twice and nobody listened.
ME: (Puts hers down too.) I'm sorry, Na. I heard but it didn't go in. Would you tell it again? This time I'll listen properly.
CON: Never mind, I don't feel like it now.
BA: Yes, if I'd told something twice and nobody listened, I wouldn't want to either. We were wrong here. So from tomorrow there's a new rule for all three of us: at dinner, phones stay on the shelf. Whoever picks one up washes the dishes.
CON: You too, Dad?
BA: If I pick it up, I wash. You're the referee.
CON: (Laughs.) Then you'll be washing dishes for a long time. ...Anyway, today Mit brought his hamster to class and it got out...
VS: Children learn screen habits by watching parents far more than by listening to them. When a parent looks at a phone while a child is speaking, the child receives a very clear unspoken message: what is in there matters more than you. Small children act up to win attention back. Older ones stop telling and turn to a screen of their own. A shared rule under which parents also bear the consequence is the most persuasive, because children are extremely sensitive to fairness.
BH: Children do not do what we say. They do what we do. If you want your child to put the phone down, put yours down first.
LN: Choose one screen-free time for everyone, starting with dinner. Set out a basket or shelf for the devices. Let your child be the referee who catches the parents.
CN: You're right, I've been holding my phone this whole time too. From tomorrow the rule is for all three of us.
TN: How many times did I open my phone in front of my child today? Check the screen-time statistics.
TN: When did I last look at my child fully while they talked, without a glance at a screen?

## Your child secretly tops up a game with your money
BC: A bank message arrives: 2.3 million dong has left Mum's account in a week, all game top-ups. Tin, in grade 7, took her phone while she showered, confirmed the codes and deleted the messages.
PX: You stole my money! It takes me days to earn 2.3 million! So young and already a thief; you'll end up in prison! | I am a thief and I will end up in prison. Mum has already ruled on it.
ME: (Calls him over, puts the phone on the table with the statement open.) Tin, I've seen these payments. I am very angry and very sad; I'm telling you that first. But I want to hear you.
CON: (Head down, a long time.) ...I topped up. I'm sorry, Mum.
ME: You admit it without denying. I note that. Now tell me: what was it for?
CON: Outfits for my character. All my friends have them. I play with the default one and they call me the poor kid.
ME: That must sting. I see why you wanted it. But I will call the way you got it by its name: taking someone's money without asking, then deleting messages to hide it. Do you know how long it takes me to earn 2.3 million?
CON: No.
ME: Nearly six working days. Six days standing at my stall from morning to night. So, how do you think this should be put right?
CON: I'll pay you back. But I don't have money.
ME: You have New Year money in your piggy bank, weekly pocket money, and two hands. Let's work it out: the piggy bank pays part, half your pocket money for ten weeks, and the rest you earn with extra chores, which I'll record. When it's paid, it's over and I won't bring it up again. And I'll remove the bank card from the phone; that part I should have done properly from the start.
CON: Okay. Do you still trust me?
ME: Trust is like a piggy bank: you broke it, so now you put coins back one at a time. I believe you can. And next time you want something, come and ask. I might say no, but we'll talk about it properly.
VS: Modern games sell items using sophisticated psychology: scarcity, comparison with friends, virtual currency that hides the real price. Twelve and thirteen-year-olds cannot yet resist these and do not yet feel the value of money. That explains the behaviour but does not erase responsibility. The effective approach is to translate the money into real working days and have the child repay with their own money and effort, with a clear end point. At the same time, close the technical door: remove the card, set a payment password.
BH: Name the act, not the person. See the repayment through, then close the matter and do not keep bringing it up.
LN: Check your phone now: is a card saved in the app store, do purchases need a password? Then tell your child once, with a real figure, how much one hour of your work earns.
CN: Trust is like a piggy bank. Once broken, we put coins back one at a time. I believe you can.
TN: Does my child understand where the family's money comes from and what we give up to earn it?
TN: When my child does wrong with money, do I give them a way to finish making it right, or do I bring it up every time I'm angry?
