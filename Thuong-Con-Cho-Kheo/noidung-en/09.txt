CHUONG: When your child is the one who wronged a friend
EPI: Doing a bad thing does not make your child a bad child. But they will only believe that if you believe it first.
LEAD: Hearing that your child is bullied hurts. Hearing that your child bullies others hurts and shames, and the first reflex is disbelief, or a punishment harsh enough to feel satisfying. Both miss the chance. A child who has hurt a friend needs to be taught the hardest thing: to look straight at what they did, to feel the other's pain, and to repair it with their own hands. This chapter also covers doubtful friends and following the crowd.

## The teacher says your child led the teasing of a classmate
BC: The form teacher asks Mum in: Nhi, in grade 5 and the class study monitor, has led a group of girls in giving a new girl nicknames, hiding her sandals and keeping others from playing with her. The girl has asked to change class. Mum cannot believe it: my child is so well behaved.
PX: Version one: There must be a mistake; my child is very good at home. Version two: I never imagined you could be so cruel. I am so disappointed in you. | Version one: Mum covers for me, so it can't be serious. Version two: I am a cruel child. That is who I am, so that is how I'll be.
ME: (In the evening, just the two of them.) Nhi, today your teacher told me about you and Hien. I want to hear it from you.
CON: We were just joking. She's weird; she dresses funny and talks with a funny accent.
ME: I hear the word joking. Let me ask: a joke is when both sides laugh. Does Hien laugh?
CON: ...No. She usually sits alone.
ME: Your teacher said Hien has asked to change class. Did you know?
CON: (Startled.) I didn't know. I didn't want it to go that far.
ME: I believe you didn't. Close your eyes and be Hien for a minute: new school, knowing no one, every morning you can't find your sandals, a whole group calling you a name you hate. How does that feel?
CON: (A long silence, eyes red.) I'd be scared to go to school.
ME: Yes. That is what Hien has felt for two months. Let me be clear: what you did is wrong, and it really hurt someone. But I do not think you are a cruel child. I think you are a child with a voice in your class; others listen to you. What can be used to harm can also be used to help.
CON: What should I do now, Mum?
ME: What do you think Hien needs most right now?
CON: Someone to play with. I'll say sorry to her. Then I'll tell the others to stop. I'll ask her to eat with us.
ME: Apologise in private, and don't make her answer straight away. And keep it up for many days, not just one. I'll check in with you every week.
VS: Children who bully are not necessarily badly behaved or starved of love. Many are respected by peers, exclude others to hold their position, and truly have not pictured the pain they cause. Denying it on the child's behalf removes the chance to make amends. Labelling the child cruel removes the reason to. The middle path separates the deed from the person, helps the child feel by standing in the other's place, then lets them propose the repair and follows it through.
BH: What you did is wrong: say it straight, without softening. Who you are, I still believe in. Those two sentences must go together.
LN: At dinner, tell about a time you once hurt a friend and how you wish you had acted. Children learn to own mistakes most easily from an adult who dares to tell their own.
CN: What you did was wrong. But I do not believe you are a bad child. I believe you can put it right.
TN: When I hear my child did wrong, am I protecting my child or my picture of a good child?
TN: At home, does my child hear adults mock others for their clothes, accent or circumstances?

## Your child goes along with the crowd
BC: A clip spreads in the parents' group: several grade 8 students surround a boy, snatch his bag and throw it around. Hoang did not snatch it, but he stands there laughing and filming.
PX: You stood there filming and could bear to watch? You are as bad as they are! | I didn't touch him and I'm counted the same. So next time I might as well join in; I get the blame anyway.
BA: Hoang, I've seen the clip. I saw you did not grab his bag. I also saw you standing, filming and laughing. What was in your head then?
CON: Nothing. They were doing it, it looked funny, so I filmed. I didn't touch him.
BA: True, you didn't. Let me ask: if you hadn't laughed but had said that's enough, what would have happened?
CON: They'd have turned on me. Said I was taking his side, and then I'd be the one teased.
BA: So you were afraid too. You laughed to stand on the safe side. I understand that fear; adults have it as well. But watch the clip again and look only at Thai's face, nobody else.
CON: (Watches, silent.) He's about to cry.
BA: To Thai in that moment, five boys grabbing the bag and ten standing and laughing were the same thing. The laughter of bystanders is what fires the others up. You don't have to be a hero and jump in. There are smaller things that still change it: don't laugh, don't film, walk off and fetch a supervisor, or afterwards go and ask Thai how he is.
CON: Asking how he is, I can do that.
BA: Then start there. And the clip: delete it, and if you sent it to anyone, message them to delete it too.
VS: Most bullying happens in front of onlookers, and their laughter or silence is the bully's reward. Children who go along are rarely malicious; they fear becoming the next target. Demanding that a child bravely intervene is too much. More realistic is a ladder of smaller acts: do not cheer, walk away, tell an adult, check on the victim afterwards. One person not laughing already changes the air.
BH: Your child need not be a hero. They only need not to be the audience. Not laughing, not filming, and checking on the friend afterwards already means standing on the right side.
LN: Ask your child: If you saw a classmate being teased by a group, what is the smallest thing you would dare to do? List three together, easy to hard. Let them choose their level.
CN: You don't have to jump in. Just don't laugh, and afterwards ask the friend how they are.
TN: At work or among relatives, when someone is being run down, do I speak up, stay quiet, or join in?
TN: Do I forward or comment on clips that humiliate people online?

## Your child's new friends worry you
BC: My, in grade 10, has grown close to a new group: dyed hair, skipping class for cafes, one already suspended. My has begun coming home late and talking differently. Mum cannot sleep.
PX: I forbid you to see them! They are all trouble. See them again and I'll keep you home from school! | Insulting my friends is insulting me. If Mum forbids it, I'll hide it. At least they don't judge me the way she does.
ME: (While driving her to the shops.) Lately I see you with Trinh's group a lot. I don't know anything about them yet. Tell me, what do you like about them?
CON: You're going to ban me, aren't you.
ME: I am worried; I won't deny it. But I know nothing yet, so I'm saying nothing yet. I'm really asking.
CON: They're fun. They don't judge whether I'm good or bad at school. With them I don't have to hold myself together.
ME: Don't have to hold yourself together. So elsewhere you're holding very tight? At school, or at home?
CON: ...Both. At school it's all a race for marks. At home you two ask about marks as well.
ME: I hear that. I will think about it, truly. As for the friends, I'm not banning you. I only have three markers that don't move: no skipping school, home by ten, and I know where you are. Within those three, your friends are your choice.
CON: That's it? You're not making me drop them?
ME: If I did you'd see them in secret, and then when something goes wrong you wouldn't dare call me. I'd rather it be in the open. And invite them over for pancakes this weekend; I want to know your friends' faces.
CON: They'd really come to our house? Don't interrogate them, okay.
ME: The only thing I'll ask is whether they want seconds.
VS: Teenagers do not choose friends at random: they go where they find what is missing, acceptance, room to breathe, being themselves. A ban touches the behaviour and ignores the need, and usually pushes the child harder towards the group, now with secrecy added. Keeping your door open to your child's friends, setting a few clear non-negotiable safety markers, and repairing what is missing at home is how you keep influence. A child with a warm tie to parents is far harder for friends to pull away.
BH: Forbid the friendship and you lose twice: they still meet, and they stop telling you. Hold a few safety markers firmly, and otherwise invite the friends home.
LN: Invite your child's friends for a simple meal. Your tasks that day: learn each name, ask nothing about marks or family background, and watch your own child among friends.
CN: Your friends are your choice. I only keep three safety markers. And I want to know your friends' faces.
TN: What is that group giving my child that they do not yet have at home?
TN: If my child got into trouble with friends at midnight, would they dare call me?

## A friend dared your child to do wrong, and your child did it
BC: The convenience store near school calls Dad: Son, in grade 7, and two friends took three chocolate bars without paying and were caught on camera. The manager has not called the police and asks a parent to come. Son stands in the corner, white as paper.
PX: Do we starve you, that you go stealing? How can I look anyone in the face! Wait till we get home! | I am a thief. Dad will never forgive me. I wish I had managed to run.
BA: (On arriving, speaks to the manager first.) I apologise. My son was wrong; we will pay and he will apologise himself. (Turns to Son, voice low.) Son, speak to him.
CON: (Barely audible.) I'm sorry, sir. I took things without paying. I was wrong.
BA: (On the way home, stops at a drinks stall.) Honestly, I'm shocked. But I saw you apologise yourself without blaming anyone. Now tell me: what happened?
CON: Luc and Binh said try taking some and see if you dare. They said whoever doesn't is chicken. I didn't want to, but I was scared they'd call me a coward.
BA: When you reached out to take it, how did you feel inside?
CON: My heart was pounding like it would fall out. I knew it was wrong.
BA: That pounding heart was the good part of you shouting. This time you didn't listen to it. You listened to two friends. I know how strong the fear of being called a coward is at your age. But notice: when you were caught, did those two step up to take the blame with you?
CON: No. They said I took it first.
BA: Right. So I have paid for the chocolate, and you will pay me back from your pocket money over four weeks. Not so I can vent, but because whoever takes, pays. And let's rehearse a line for the next dare: I don't play that game. Say it to me.
CON: I don't play that game.
BA: Louder. Say it like you believe it.
VS: Petty group theft in early adolescence is usually not about the item but about a test of nerve in front of friends. Fear of exclusion at this age is strong enough to drown out the sense of right and wrong the child already has. Calling the child a thief is therefore both wrong and dangerous: it hands them a new identity. What is needed is for the child to face real consequences, apologise, repay from their own money, and be equipped with a refusal line rehearsed in advance.
BH: Let your child apologise and repay in person. Real consequences teach more than any beating. And never call your child by the name of the mistake they have just made.
LN: With your child, write three ways to refuse friends without losing face, in their own voice: I don't play that game. Nah, I'm going home. My dad would kill me. Practise aloud. A refusal must already be in the mouth before it is needed.
CN: That pounding heart was the good part of you shouting. Next time, listen to it.
TN: Is my child confident enough to bear being called a coward? What helps build that confidence?
TN: When my child does wrong, do I let them face the consequence, or do I smooth it over for them?
