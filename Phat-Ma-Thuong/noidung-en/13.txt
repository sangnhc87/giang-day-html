CHUONG: When not to punish: when the student is in trouble
EPI: Sometimes the wisest answer a teacher can give is: today I will not punish, I will ask how you are.
LEAD: After many chapters of punishing moves, this chapter says the opposite. Wrong behaviour is sometimes not worth punishing but worth asking about. A student who is crying, behaving unusually, erring because of trouble at home, or owning up by themselves: these are times when punishment damages more than it builds. Moreover, there are times when the teacher is the one who has spoken too harshly. This chapter teaches when to use a punishing move and when to use a no-punishment move.

## A student is crying and not coping
LOI: You have reminded Phuong several times for not doing her work; she sits silent. When you approach, her eyes are red and tears are falling on her notebook. She says nothing. The class begins to look.
CHIEU: The exemption slip | Keep a stack of exemption slips; each student has two per term. Lay one gently on her desk and say very softly: today you use this slip; I will not mention the work again. She may step out to wash her face if she likes. At the end of the lesson you ask privately how she is. The slip needs no explanation and no asking. She knows that when today is not okay there is a way out.
THAY: (Softly.) Phuong, today you use this exemption slip. I will not mention the work any more. Go and wash your face if you wish, I will wait.
TRO: (Nods, tears falling.) Yes...
THAY: At the end of the lesson, if you want to talk, I am there. If not, that is fine too.
VS: Punishing a student who is crying is striking one who has fallen. The exemption slip gives her a legitimate right to be not okay without losing face. She will remember this kindness far longer than anything in that lesson.
RG: The slip is not to be abused, but if she uses up her slips early, that is a sign she needs more attention. Do not press for the cause while she is crying. If there are signs of risk of self-harm, tell family and school immediately.
BT: Keep a box of tissues on your desk that anyone may take from without asking.
CN: Today you use the exemption slip. I will not mention the work, and I am here if you need me.

## A student owns up before you ask
LOI: After break, Tan steps up to your desk: sir, I broke the vase at the back of the room. He stands there, hands trembling. You know it took great courage to say it.
CHIEU: Rewarding the one who dares to speak | Stop what you are doing, look at him and say slowly: thank you for telling me. To be honest, I am more glad of your honesty than sorry for the vase. Ask: did you cut yourself? You deal with the vase; he only helps you sweep up the pieces. No extra punishment. Keep a private wink: next time just tell the truth like that.
THAY: Thank you for telling me. I am more glad of your honesty than sorry for the vase. Did you cut your hand? Let us sweep up together, no further punishment.
TRO: Um... I thought you would be angry.
THAY: I am not angry. One who dares own up is one I trust.
VS: Punishing the one who owns up teaches him to hide next time. Praising honesty and dropping the penalty teaches that telling the truth does not hurt. A student respected for owning up will own up for life.
RG: Be consistent: do not punish this student while not punishing another for a similar fault when they did not own up. If the fault is large, still follow regulations but reduce the penalty according to attitude. Do not praise so much that he feels he must err to be praised.
BT: Keep a record of honest admissions and at the end of the year hand out thank-you cards.
CN: Thank you for telling me; I am more glad of your honesty than sorry for the vase.

## A student keeps erring because of trouble at home
LOI: You notice Binh lately is behind with work, often dozes, snaps at friends. Another student quietly says his parents are moving out of the family home. He has not said anything to you.
CHIEU: The three-line notebook | Do not ask directly about home. Put a small notebook in front of him and say: each morning you may write three lines: how you are today, what is hard, what I can help with. Only I read it. I reply with one line, not much comment. Small faults in this period you quietly do not count, and keep watching. When he is ready, he opens up himself.
THAY: Binh, I have a small notebook for you. Each morning you can write three lines: how you are today, what is hard, what I can help with. Only I read it.
TRO: Um... I can take it, sir.
THAY: Write whatever you like. I just read it and answer in one line.
VS: He is carrying something big, so small faults are only signs. More punishment is more weight. The notebook gives him a way to speak when the mouth cannot yet, and you play the role of a safe listener.
RG: Do not press him to talk about home. Do not gossip with colleagues beyond those who need to know, such as the school and psychological support. If there are signs he is in danger or abused, tell those responsible at once.
BT: When he does not want to write, change it to three symbols: a smiling face, a sad face, a flat face.
CN: Write me three lines each morning. Only I read them, and I am here.

## A student misbehaves to get attention
LOI: Cuong is always the naughtiest in the class: loud, teasing friends, provoking you. Every kind of punishment has failed to reduce it; the more he is punished, the naughtier he gets. You suddenly wonder what he is lacking.
CHIEU: Catch him doing good | Reverse the strategy. Set a secret goal: each day catch Cuong doing one good thing and tell him privately. It might be handing a pen to a friend, arriving on time, answering correctly. Say it privately, one short line: I saw you do something good. After a few weeks, he gets attention through good deeds, and acting up for attention fades.
THAY: (Privately, after the lesson.) Cuong, today I saw you hand your pen to a friend without being asked. I think you are kind.
TRO: Um... you really saw that, sir?
THAY: I really did. I see a lot of things; I just have not said so.
VS: He misbehaves for attention, and if bad behaviour is the only way to get it, he will keep doing it. Redirecting attention to good deeds gives him a better road to the same goal. Private praise is also more believable.
RG: Praise must be real, not invented. Do not use praise as a prize that makes him perform. Keep clear limits on wrong behaviour; do not drop punishment altogether.
BT: Invite him to be your assistant for some small task, so good deeds have an outlet.
CN: Today I saw you do something good, and I want you to know I saw it.

## The teacher said something too harsh
LOI: In the middle of the lesson, because he is tired and hot, you snap at Dung: do you have any manners? The class falls silent. Dung bows his head, eyes red. You realise you went too far. After the lesson, you sit alone thinking it over.
CHIEU: The teacher takes his own punishment | Next morning at the start of class, ask the class's permission to say something. Say directly: yesterday I spoke harshly to a student, and I was wrong. I apologise to him before the class, or in private if he prefers. Punish yourself: do the cleaning duty for him for a day or tell a story of a time you were wrong. The class sees that you too err, that you dare own it, and your standing rises, not falls.
THAY: Dung, yesterday I spoke harshly to you. I was wrong. I am sorry. I will not excuse myself; I only want you to know I do not think what I said.
TRO: (Silent a moment.) Um... thank you, sir.
THAY: I owe you something. Tell me what I can do to make up for it, and I will do it.
VS: Students remember how a teacher owns a fault longer than they remember the fault. A genuine apology teaches the whole class about responsibility and gives Dung fairness. Teachers too need the right to err and mend.
RG: Do not apologise just to be done and then repeat it. Do not shift blame indirectly, such as I apologise but you had a part too. Do not make the apology a burden requiring him to forgive at once.
BT: Keep a weekly habit of self-review: this week, did I speak harshly at any moment, and how did I mend it.
CN: Yesterday I spoke harshly. I was wrong. I am sorry.
