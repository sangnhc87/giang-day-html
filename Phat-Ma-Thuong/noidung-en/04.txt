CHUONG: Dozing, drowsy, tired at the start of the day
EPI: Before calling a student lazy, ask how many hours he slept last night.
LEAD: Dozing is the fault that makes teachers angriest, and also the one with the most real causes: late-night study, gaming, helping at home, part-time work, or simply an adolescence that needs more sleep than an adult. This chapter offers gentle, light ways to wake a student, and, more importantly, ways to find the real reason.
## A student drops his head on the desk mid-lesson
LOI: Long has had his head on the desk since the fifteenth minute. You call once and he lifts it; a few minutes later it is down again. The class is starting to glance over.
CHIEU: The standing desk at the back | Say gently: Long, you are very sleepy. I will let you go to the back and study standing for five minutes. A tall desk is kept ready at the back. He stands there holding his notebook. Five minutes later he returns to his seat. Nobody is named or laughed at, because you have said it is a place for anyone who needs to stay alert. At the end of the lesson ask him privately what time he went to sleep last night.
THAY: Long, I can see you are very tired. Go to the standing desk at the back and study there for a few minutes to wake up. It is not a punishment, it is a place I keep for whoever needs it.
TRO: Um... thank you, sir.
THAY: At the end of the lesson I will ask you something small; see me then.
VS: Waking him in front of the class shames him and he is still sleepy. Standing changes his posture, sends blood to the brain, and makes the spot a right, not a penalty. The private question afterwards is what touches the cause.
RG: Do not use it for a student who is ill or faint. Do not keep the standing desk as a fixed place of punishment. If he sleeps every lesson, every day, think of health or family problems.
BT: Give the whole class a one-minute stretch whenever you see everyone slumping, so the standing desk is not a signal for one student.
CN: Go and stand at the back for a few minutes to wake up. At the end of the lesson I will ask you something.

## A student dozes from gaming late at night
LOI: The dark circles say it all. Kiet admits he played until two in the morning. He dozes almost every first lesson. His parents have told him and he does not listen.
CHIEU: Three-day sleep log | Give Kiet a small challenge: for three days in a row, write his real bedtime on a slip, and next morning rate his alertness from one to ten. You only look; you do not comment. After three days ask what he sees. Usually he finds the link between sleep and alertness himself. If he keeps to bed before eleven for two days, you give a small reward, like changing his seat.
THAY: Kiet, I am not asking you to quit gaming. I only ask you to log your bedtime for three days, and in the morning rate your alertness one to ten. Then we see what it says.
TRO: Um... just write it down, sir?
THAY: Just write it down. I do not grade it, I am only curious what the numbers will tell.
VS: Tell him to quit gaming and he resists. Recording his sleep lets him see cause and effect for himself. Students of this age remember what they discover themselves far longer than what adults tell them.
RG: Do not confiscate his device or ban gaming in place of his parents. Do not read the log aloud. If he games through the night repeatedly, invite his parents to talk it through together.
BT: Add a mood column so he sees sleep affecting his feelings too.
CN: I am not asking you to quit gaming. Log your bedtime for three days and we will see what it tells us.

## A student dozes because of work at home
LOI: Mai dozes almost every morning. You know her family sells food at night and she helps until late. She never complains, only apologises whenever she is woken.
CHIEU: The ten-minute rest corner | This is a no-punishment move. Tell Mai privately: if you are too tired, go out and wash your face for ten minutes; you need no permission, just nod to me. Put a small chair at the end of the corridor near the room for her to sit. Quietly ask the class monitor not to note her name when she steps out. Write a private note in your own notebook for when a talk with the school or family is needed.
THAY: Mai, I know your family works hard. When you are too tired, just nod to me and go and wash your face for ten minutes. I will not ask why.
TRO: (Eyes red.) Um... thank you, sir.
THAY: You have done nothing wrong. I only want you to learn as much as you can within what you are able to.
VS: Punishing her for tiredness caused by family work is unjust and shames her for her circumstances. A discreet arrangement lets her keep her dignity and still keep up with the lesson.
RG: Do not reveal her circumstances to the class. Do not press her to tell more if she is not ready. If she truly needs support, quietly contact the school and support funds.
BT: Give her a summary of the lesson at the end so she does not miss content when she steps out.
CN: When you are too tired, just nod to me and go and wash your face. I will not ask why.

## The whole class yawns in a chain
LOI: Third lesson of the morning; one student yawns, then the whole row follows like a chain. You are mid-explanation and see a glazed class. Nobody yawns on purpose; they are simply tired.
CHIEU: One minute to wake up | Stop teaching and smile: is the whole class yawning with me? Give the command: stand up, stretch as wide as you can, yawn as big as you like once, then sit and take a sip of water. After a minute, ask who feels more awake to raise a hand. Note the number. This punishes no one but turns drowsiness into a short, fun group activity.
THAY: Everyone stand up! Stretch wide, yawn as big as you can once, as much as you like. Then sit and drink water. Who feels more awake, raise your hand.
TRO: (Laughing, the class does it.) Yes!
THAY: Good. Now we carry on, and this time more awake.
VS: A chain of yawns is a social reflex. Letting the class do it in a controlled way eases the drowsiness, and laughing helps them relax. You are seen as someone who understands the class instead of someone who forbids.
RG: Do not force anyone who is truly unwell to stand. Do not let it run beyond two minutes.
BT: Add a four-count breathing exercise: in for four, hold four, out four.
CN: Everyone stand and yawn as big as you like once, and then we go on with the lesson.

## A student is sleepy straight after PE
LOI: In the lesson after PE, Nam sits dazed, eyes dull, shirt still damp with sweat. He is not following the lesson. The class is sluggish with him.
CHIEU: The teacher's cup of water | Keep a jug of water ready on your desk. At the start of the lesson after PE, pour each student a small sip while asking a fun question: what did you play, did you win or lose? The class chats for about three minutes. Then all take three deep breaths and start the lesson. The punishment lies in not letting their bodies be forgotten: you answer inattention with care.
THAY: A sip of water for everyone. Who played what, who won, tell me in three minutes. Then we take deep breaths and begin.
TRO: Yes, we played football, our class won!
THAY: Very good. Let the winners rest now, and we return to the maths problem.
VS: After exercise the body needs time to settle. Demanding attention at once asks for what is not ready. A sip of water and a few minutes of chat help the whole class change state gently.
RG: Do not offer water to students who must avoid it for health reasons. Do not let the chat grow into noise.
BT: Use two minutes of sitting with eyes closed to quiet music in place of the chat.
CN: Let us drink some water, rest three minutes and then begin.

GN: Do not call out a dozing student in front of the class. A light touch on the shoulder, a tap on the desk as you pass, or a class stretch first.
GN: Every dozing student has a reason of their own. One private question after the lesson is worth ten public reminders.
GN: A student who keeps dozing for a long time is usually a signal to the teacher, not a fault in the student. Note it and speak to the family when needed.
