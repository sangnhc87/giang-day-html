CHUONG: When you are the one boiling over: steady yourself first
EPI: Children do not remember everything we say when we are angry. They remember our face.
LEAD: Most of the sentences that hurt a child deepest are spoken in the first ninety seconds of anger. Not because parents do not love, but because in those seconds the thinking part of the mind steps aside for the part that only reacts. This chapter does not tell you never to be angry. Anger is human. It deals with one thing: how to let anger pass without leaving behind a sentence you will regret.

## Your child breaks the vase you treasured
BC: Na, in grade 3, is running around the living room and knocks over the ceramic vase her mother brought back from her last trip with Grandma. It breaks into three pieces. Na freezes, eyes on her mother, lips pressed tight.
PX: How many times have I told you! Why are you so clumsy? | I am a clumsy child. The vase matters more than I do. Next time I break something, I had better hide it.
ME: (Takes a breath, kneels to her level.) Did you cut your hand anywhere? Let me see.
CON: No... I'm sorry, Mum. I didn't mean to.
ME: I know you didn't. I'll be honest, I feel very sad, because I bought this vase when I was with Grandma. Give me a minute.
CON: (Bursts into tears.) I broke Grandma's thing...
ME: You are crying because you care about the vase too; I can see that. I am sad that it broke, but I have not stopped loving you. Let's clean up together. You get the broom; I'll pick up the big pieces.
CON: Mum, can we glue it back?
ME: We can try. It won't look as good as before, but I'll keep it on the shelf. Now, about running in the living room: what do you think we should do from tomorrow?
CON: I'll play outside. Inside, I'll walk slowly.
VS: A child who has just caused an accident is already frightened. If an adult shouts on top of that, fear takes up all the room and there is nothing left for thinking about what happened. The child only defends: denies, blames, or stands frozen. When a parent first asks whether the child is hurt, the child understands she is still safe, and only then can real remorse appear.
BH: What is broken is broken. Your job in that minute is to make sure nothing else breaks with it.
LN: Choose one sentence to say whenever your child breaks or spills something, such as: Are you all right? Say it aloud three times. Only a sentence practised in advance will come out when you are hot.
CN: Things can be fixed or bought again. There is only one of you.
TN: When I broke something as a child, what did the adults say? Do I still remember how that felt?
TN: When my child does something wrong, do they run to tell me, or try to hide it?

## Dad comes home exhausted and the whining starts
BC: Dad has just been told off by his boss and sat in traffic for nearly an hour. The moment he opens the door, Bin, in grade 2, tugs his shirt to show a model he built, then whines for a new set. Dad feels the blood rush to his face.
PX: Quiet! I'm dead tired and you keep nagging. Go play somewhere else! | Dad does not want to see me. I waited for him all afternoon, and that was wrong.
BA: Bin, I can see you've been waiting for me. I'll tell you the truth: I am very tired today and my head is hot. It is not because of you.
CON: But look at this, I spent the whole afternoon building it!
BA: I want to look at it properly, not in a rush. Give me fifteen minutes to shower and drink some water. When the long hand reaches 6, I'll come out. Leave it right there on the table.
CON: Fifteen minutes is so long.
BA: Yes, waiting does feel long. Can you watch the clock for me? If time is up and I'm not out, come and knock.
CON: (Fifteen minutes later.) Dad, time's up!
BA: Here I am. Now tell me, how did you attach this wing so it doesn't fall off?
VS: Small children cannot read the reason behind an adult's face. Seeing Dad scowl, the child concludes it is his fault. When a parent says plainly, I am tired and it is not because of you, the child is released from guilt. Naming a specific time and then keeping it teaches something large: waiting is possible, because a parent's word can be trusted.
BH: You do not have to be available every moment. You only have to say honestly that you are not ready yet, and name a time when you will be fully there.
LN: Today, before you walk in the door, stand outside for one minute. Take three long breaths. Ask: whose problem is this bad mood? Then leave as much of it outside as you can.
CN: I'm tired, and it is not because of you. Give me fifteen minutes and then I'm all yours.
TN: Who at home usually receives the frustration I bring back from work?
TN: When did I last tell my child in a minute and then forget?

## You shouted at your child in front of others
BC: A family memorial gathering, relatives everywhere. Vy, in grade 7, is buried in her phone and does not answer when her uncle asks twice. Mum, embarrassed, scolds her loudly at the table. Vy gets up and walks out. That evening her door stays shut.
PX: That was mild. You brought it on yourself by embarrassing me in front of the whole family. | Mum's reputation matters more than I do. I was humiliated and I am still the one at fault.
ME: (Knocks.) Vy, may I come in? I'm not here to scold you more.
CON: ...Come in.
ME: I am sorry about lunchtime. I shouted at you in front of everyone. That was wrong of me.
CON: I was so embarrassed. My cousins were looking at me and laughing.
ME: If that happened to me I would want to disappear too. I felt awkward in front of your uncle, I got angry, and I took it out on you. I should have taken you aside.
CON: I was wrong too. I really didn't hear him; I was in the middle of texting a friend.
ME: Thank you for saying that. So let's agree: next time something isn't right, I'll touch your shoulder once and say nothing. Will you understand?
CON: Okay. Just don't make it a pinch.
VS: For young teenagers, saving face in front of others feels almost like survival. Corrected in private, a child hears the content. Corrected in public, the child hears only shame, and shame turns quickly into resentment. A parent who apologises for the way they spoke loses no authority. They are showing, live, what an adult owning a mistake looks like.
BH: Correct in private. And if you have shamed your child in front of others, apologise properly, with no but attached.
LN: Agree on a private signal for crowded places: a touch on the shoulder, a harmless phrase. Let your child choose the signal; they will remember it better.
CN: Shouting at you in front of everyone was my mistake. I am sorry.
TN: When my child embarrasses me, am I worried about my child or about my image?
TN: Have I ever apologised to my child without adding because you...?

## The anger is at your throat and your hand is raised
BC: Khoa, in grade 5, said his homework was done; a message from the teacher reveals it was not. Questioned, he argues back. Dad raises his hand. Khoa shrinks and shuts his eyes. That flinch is what stops Dad.
PX: You won't learn unless you're hit! My father beat me harder than this, and I turned out fine. | Whoever is stronger is right. Next time I must lie more cleverly so I don't get hit.
BA: (Lowers his hand, steps back.) I am too angry. If I keep talking now I will say and do the wrong thing. I'm going out for ten minutes. Stay here; when I come back we will talk.
CON: (Silent, eyes red.)
BA: (Returns, pulls up a chair.) I raised my hand just now and scared you. I do not want that to happen again. The homework and the lie still have to be talked about, but in a different way.
CON: I thought you were going to hit me.
BA: I nearly did. I am not proud of that. Now I am asking for real, not to catch you out: why did you say it was done when it wasn't?
CON: Because I don't know how to do it. Fractions. I was scared you'd call me stupid if I told you.
BA: So you are more afraid of me than of the homework. That is mine to fix. Get your notebook and we'll look at the first problem. And tomorrow morning you'll tell your teacher the truth yourself; I'll stand beside you.
VS: Hitting stops behaviour instantly, which is why adults believe it works. But what the child learns is not do not lie; it is do not get caught. Many long-term studies find that children who are hit do not behave better over time; they tend to be more aggressive, more anxious and more secretive. Leaving the room for a few minutes is not losing to your child. It is an adult using the brakes.
BH: The moment your hand wants to rise is the moment your feet should walk out. Teach later, when you are yourself again.
LN: Tell the family a rule in advance: when Mum or Dad says I need ten minutes, nobody holds them back, and the one who said it must return to finish the conversation that same day. Write it down and stick it on the fridge.
CN: I am too angry, so I am stepping out for a moment. I will come back and we will talk properly.
TN: What sign in my body tells me I am about to lose control: a hot face, clenched teeth, a rising voice?
TN: The beating I remember best from childhood: do I remember the lesson or the fear?
