CHUONG: Adolescent feelings: crushes, love notes, gifts, confessions
EPI: Liking someone is not a fault. A fault arises only when the feeling makes a student forget he is in class, or hurts someone else.
LEAD: Every teacher meets it: two students exchange smiles, one passes a note, one brings food for a friend, one confesses in the middle of the class and everyone hoots. Forbid it absolutely and they hide it, and hiding is when things most easily go wrong. Threaten to tell parents and they close the door on you. This chapter offers moves that keep the lesson's rhythm and respect for both sides without turning feelings into a crime. The limits are especially important, because there are signs at which you must drop the gentle move and protect the student.

## You notice two students have feelings for each other
LOI: You notice Tram and Khoi often glance at each other, sit close, laugh at things only the two understand. Their marks have not dropped but they are often distracted. The class begins to whisper and pair them up.
CHIEU: A story from the teacher's youth | Do not call the two up to ask. In a homeroom period, tell a funny story about the time you liked a classmate at high school: you cycled past her house ten times and never dared to stop once. The class laughs. Say: liking someone is perfectly normal, nobody is at fault. A fault arises only when you neglect your work, your friends, or embarrass the other person. You point at nobody. The two listen and understand that you know, that you do not judge, and that you hope they keep both things.
THAY: Let me tell you something: in high school I cycled past a friend's house ten times and never dared stop. Liking someone is normal. I only hope we keep studying well and stay kind to one another.
TRO: (The class laughs.) And then what happened, sir?
THAY: Then I studied extremely hard so I would have something to tell. Right, back to the lesson.
VS: Feelings that are scrutinised turn into secrets, and at this age secrets lead students to places teachers cannot reach. A funny story normalises the emotion, cools the class's teasing, and tells the two that you are someone to talk to when needed.
RG: Do not point at the two. Do not publicly pair them. Do not read diaries or messages. If you see signs a student is coerced, pursued by a much older person, or behaving intimately beyond their age, drop this move and report to the school and family as regulations require.
BT: Invite a young teacher or a counsellor to talk to the whole year about friendship, love and boundaries, so the homeroom teacher is not the only one.
CN: Liking someone is normal. We just need to study well and be kind to each other.

## A student passes notes or writes love letters in class
LOI: In the middle of the lesson, a note folded in four passes across three desks. You intercept it. Opening it, you see it is a love letter from Dung to Ha, written very awkwardly. Dung's face goes bright red.
CHIEU: The teacher keeps the letter and returns it quietly | Do not read it aloud. Fold it, put it in your pocket and say softly: I will keep this for you and return it at the end. At the end, return it to Dung privately, not mentioning the content, saying only: writing a letter is a lovely thing, but lesson time is for the lesson. Give him a small slip reading: love letters are for break or after school. Add: you will write better if you dare read it aloud to a close friend for advice before sending.
THAY: (Quietly, passing the desk.) I will keep this slip of paper for you and give it back at the end of the lesson.
TRO: (At the end, red-faced.) Um... did you read it, sir?
THAY: I did not read it, and I will tell no one. Just remember: letters are for break, and lesson time is for the lesson.
VS: Intercepting the note is needed to keep the lesson's rhythm, but shaming him makes the embarrassment last. Keeping it private, returning it in confidence and not reading it shows you respect his privacy. He remembers that, and will find it easier to tell the truth next time.
RG: Do not read aloud, do not display him before the class, do not call his parents over one letter. If the content shows signs of harassment or threat, or the letter comes from a much older person, deal with it seriously and protect the recipient.
BT: Open a small mailbox in a corner of the class for encouragement and thank-you letters, so students have an outlet for writing well.
CN: I will keep it for you and give it back at the end. Letters are for break, and lesson time is for the lesson.

## A student brings food or gifts for the one he likes
LOI: Every morning Tuan leaves a carton of milk or a cake on Vy's desk. Vy is flustered, not daring to accept nor to refuse. The class watches. Some days Vy gives it to another student, and Tuan is visibly annoyed.
CHIEU: The three-gift rule | Invite Tuan for a private talk and say gently: caring for a friend is good, but I need you to understand three small rules. One, small gifts only, and do not oblige her to accept. Two, give outside lesson time, and only when she agrees. Three, if she declines, say thank you and stop, without being angry. Add privately to Vy: you have the right to refuse; saying no politely is something you are allowed to do.
THAY: Tuan, I understand you want to look after her. We have three small rules: small gifts only, give outside lesson time and when she agrees, and if she says no we say thank you and stop. Fair?
TRO: Um... if she says no I will be very sad, sir.
THAY: The sadness is real, I understand. But refusing is also her right, and respecting that right is how you show you are growing up.
VS: A steady stream of gifts easily becomes pressure on the recipient. The three rules help him learn to show affection while respecting the other's consent. Speaking privately to Vy lets her know she has the right to refuse without being thought rude.
RG: Do not publicise the gifts. Do not make Vy accept or refuse before the class. If the gifts are expensive, or come with clinging and monitoring, it is no longer light affection; talk seriously and inform the family.
BT: Teach the whole class one polite way to refuse: thank you, but I would rather not accept, then role-play it for three minutes.
CN: Caring for a friend is good, but she has the right to say no. We say thank you and stop.

## A student confesses his love publicly before the class
LOI: At break, Phat stands up in the middle of the room, hands Ngoc a bouquet of paper flowers and says loudly: I like you. The class goes oh, then claps and whoops. Ngoc bows her head, refuses softly, and the whole class turns to tease the two.
CHIEU: Three lines of respect | Walk in, smile and raise a hand for quiet. Say: Phat just did something brave, telling the truth to someone he likes. Ngoc also did something brave, telling the truth about her feelings. Ask the class to repeat the three lines you write on the board: respect the one who speaks, respect the one who refuses, and tease nobody today. Afterwards meet Phat privately, saying that being refused does not make him any less, and meet Ngoc privately, saying she has done nothing wrong.
THAY: Phat bravely told the truth. Ngoc bravely told the truth. All of you read these three lines with me: respect the one who speaks, respect the one who refuses, and tease nobody today.
TRO: (The class reads along, a few laugh.) Yes!
THAY: Now give the two some space. We go into the lesson. I will talk to Phat and Ngoc privately shortly.
VS: A public confession puts both in a difficult spot, and the class's whooping can become pressure or humiliation. Lifting both as brave, with a collective agreement, turns the class from teasing to respect. Meeting separately lets each be cared for.
RG: Do not punish Phat for confessing, and do not make Ngoc explain. If the class keeps mocking either one, treat it as insulting teasing. If afterwards either shows decline or wants to leave school from embarrassment, watch closely and support.
BT: At the end of the week the class writes one shared card to the two: whatever happens, we are still friends.
CN: Telling your feelings honestly is brave, and refusing politely is brave too. We respect both.

## Two students are too affectionate in class
LOI: Two students sit pressed together, hold hands under the desk, lean on each other's shoulder during the lesson. Those around them are uncomfortable. One complains of being unable to concentrate; another films it for fun.
CHIEU: A dictionary apart | Meet the two privately, speaking gently and lightly: I believe your feelings are lovely. In class we have a fun yardstick: always a dictionary's width between you. This week put an actual dictionary on the desk between them. Explain clearly: it is not that feelings are bad, but that the classroom is a shared workplace and those around need to be comfortable. Outside lessons, the yard also has its limits, according to school rules.
THAY: I believe your feelings are lovely. In class we have a fun yardstick: a dictionary between you. This week I put a real one in the middle, all right?
TRO: (Laughing awkwardly.) Um... thick or thin, sir?
THAY: Thick. Because I want you to do well at school even while in love. At break you have your own space, but still within school rules.
VS: A private talk in a light tone lets the two listen without defending. The dictionary is an easily remembered, easily followed symbol, and shows that you are not banning feelings, only keeping the standard of a place of study.
RG: Do not shame the two before the class. Do not use words like depraved or shameless. If the behaviour goes beyond what is normal at school age, or shows signs of unsafe contact or coercion, report at once to the school and family and support the student with safety knowledge.
BT: Replace the dictionary with something the two choose, and sign with you a short agreement on where and when to show affection.
CN: Your feelings are lovely. In class we keep a dictionary's width apart.

## Two students break up; the class is drawn in and one goes downhill
LOI: After the breakup, Huong cries a lot, misses some sessions, then comes back with a dazed face. Her ex, Quan, is cold; a few classmates take sides, whisper, and send messages slandering each other.
CHIEU: Three breaths and a chair | Do not call the two up to reconcile them. Do two things. For the class: for several lessons, begin with three deep breaths and mention a light convention: what is between two friends belongs to the two. For Huong: put a chair at the end of the corridor and tell her privately: when it gets too heavy, go and sit there for three minutes, no permission needed, and I will check on you after the lesson. Quietly ease her workload this week.
THAY: Huong, whenever it feels too heavy, go and sit on the chair at the end of the corridor for three minutes, no need to ask me. After the lesson I will check on you; talk if you want, and if not, that is fine.
TRO: (Eyes red.) Um... thank you, sir.
THAY: Everyone goes through this, and it eases gradually. I am here.
VS: A breakup at this age is a real shock, sometimes heavier than adults think. Letting her step back without losing face and easing the pressure temporarily helps her through. Stopping the class from commenting protects both.
RG: Do not force the two to make up or stay friends. Do not look at their messages. If she shows signs of depression, skipping meals, self-harm or talks of vanishing, tell family, school and a counsellor at once. Never treat it as small.
BT: Introduce her to someone she can talk to, such as the school counsellor, if she seems to need more support.
CN: Whenever it is too heavy, sit on the chair at the end of the corridor, and I will check on you after the lesson.
