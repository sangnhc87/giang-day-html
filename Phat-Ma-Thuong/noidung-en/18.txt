CHUONG: School yard and shared activities: flag salute, queues, corridors, the gate
EPI: Collective routine is where students learn to be part of a crowd and still be themselves.
LEAD: Not every fault happens in the classroom. Slack flag salutes, queue-jumping at the canteen, thundering along corridors, speeding at the gate, dodging labour duty, noise while another class sits an exam: faults hard to punish because they happen among crowds and teachers see them only in passing. This chapter offers light moves, usable on the spot or in a private talk afterwards, for activities outside the classroom.

## A student chats and fidgets during the flag salute
LOI: In the middle of the flag salute, a group at the back of the line giggles, shoves, and one sits down. You are far away and cannot remind them in time. The whole line standing to attention is affected.
CHIEU: The statue pose | The next day, take the first three minutes of homeroom for a quick statue game: whoever moves first is out. Have the whole class try standing as a statue for one minute. The winner receives the title of finest statue of the week. Then say: the flag salute is the same; standing still for a short time is a strength. The class sets its own goal: this week the whole class stands like statues through two songs.
THAY: Let us play statues: whoever moves first sits down. One minute, begin! ... Who is the finest statue this week? The flag salute is also a statue contest; let us try to stand still through two songs.
TRO: (Laughing.) Yes, we can do it!
THAY: I believe in our class. Next Monday I will be watching.
VS: Keeping order during the salute is hard from impatience, not from disrespect. Turning standing still into a game gives a concrete goal. Praising the class before the salute works better than reproaching after a bad one.
RG: Do not punish the whole class for a few. Do not make them stand beyond their ability, especially in blazing sun. A student who faints or tires must be moved to shade at once.
BT: Each week pick a finest statue, voted by the students themselves.
CN: Let us play statues: stand still for a minute, then we try the same at the flag salute.

## A student jumps the queue at the canteen
LOI: At break, a group of senior boys push to the front of the canteen line, shoving the younger ones back. The younger ones quietly give way. You see it but do not know who.
CHIEU: Honorary last in line | Do not catch anyone in the act. The next day, invite the queue-jumpers to stand at the back of the line for a week, and call it honorary last in line. Truly last: they stand at the very end, letting younger children go first. Ask: how does it feel to let others go before you? A week later, ask again. Students at the end of the line often say they felt bigger.
THAY: This week you are honorary last in line at the canteen: stand at the very back, let the younger ones go first. At the end of the week I will ask how it felt.
TRO: Um... waiting at the back takes ages, sir.
THAY: Yes, a few more minutes. But the younger children are very grateful. I will ask at the end of the week.
VS: Queue-jumping usually comes from hurry and the habit of the older pushing the younger. Being last in line reverses the position from taker to giver, and the students feel they hold good power. The title helps them keep face.
RG: Do not publicise their names before the whole school. Do not let the younger ones be pushed to the point of fear. If the jumping is bullying, treat it as bullying.
BT: Add a group of older students as queue ambassadors, guiding the younger ones.
CN: This week you are honorary last in line at the canteen, letting the younger ones go first.

## A student thunders along corridors and stairs
LOI: The bell rings and the whole year pours into the corridor. Someone runs downstairs two steps at a time, pushing, sliding down the banister. A student nearly falls. You shout, but your shout is lost in the din.
CHIEU: Feet worth a thousand gold | Do not shout. Call the group who often run aside and say: I give you a special mission: for three days you walk the corridor with feet worth a thousand gold, slow and light as if carrying a full cup of water. You stand at the end of the corridor and watch. Whoever does it right, you nod. Whoever runs is not punished, only goes back to the top of the stairs and walks again. The corridor becomes a stage for calm.
THAY: For the next three days you walk the corridor as if carrying a full cup of water, light and slow. I will stand at the end of the corridor. Whoever does it right gets a nod from me.
TRO: Um... we will try, sir.
THAY: Whoever slips and runs just goes back to the top of the stairs and walks again; no harm done.
VS: Running in corridors is energy built up after lessons. The light-step challenge turns it into a difficult game, and you stand watching rather than shouting. Going back and walking again is an instant consequence without shame.
RG: Do not make them go so slowly they block the way at peak times. If a student is hurt or there is clear risk, step in at once. Do not punish a one-off lapse.
BT: Give the whole year a quietest-corridor contest, hanging a sign in the winning class.
CN: For three days you walk the corridor as if carrying a full cup of water.

## A student speeds and parks wrongly at the school gate
LOI: At the end of the day, a student on an electric bike weaves through the crowd and nearly hits a younger child. The gate is jammed. Some students park blocking the entrance.
CHIEU: A shift as traffic marshal | Meet the speeding student privately and say plainly but gently: I am worried for you and for the others. Invite him to join the school's traffic monitors at going-home time for three days, standing at the gate, reminding others to go slowly and guiding parking. He sees the crowd from a coordinator's viewpoint. If he still speeds, you will ask his family to remind him together.
THAY: I am worried for you and for the younger children. For the next three days you are honorary traffic marshal at the gate at going-home time: stand at the gate and remind everyone to go slowly.
TRO: Um... I can do that, sir.
THAY: Thank you. You will see what the gate looks like at going-home time from the marshal's side.
VS: A speeding student seldom sees the risk to others. Standing as coordinator gives an entirely different viewpoint. Being given a duty makes him want to be a model.
RG: You must inform the family if he rides dangerously, especially if he is below the legal driving age. Do not let him stand in the road. The duty takes place only on school grounds under supervision.
BT: Invite the local police or a traffic specialist to give a short talk to the whole year on safety.
CN: For three days you are honorary traffic marshal at the gate, reminding everyone to go slowly.

## A student dodges labour duty and group activities
LOI: At the class labour session, Son stands in a corner, phone in hand, just watching classmates sweep the yard. When reminded he says: I am not doing anything and they do not need me anyway.
CHIEU: The referee post | Do not make him hold a broom. Give him a different post: labour referee. He carries a notebook, recording what each group has done and anything done well, and at the end of the session reads out three compliments to the groups. He may also suggest how to share the work more sensibly. At the next session, ask: would you like to try joining a group? Often he asks to join himself, having seen the effort clearly.
THAY: Son, I give you the post of labour referee: note what each group has done, and at the end read three compliments to the groups. Will you do that for me?
TRO: Um... I will be the referee, yes.
THAY: Thank you. Next time, if you want to try a group, just tell me.
VS: A student standing outside usually feels he does not belong. The referee post gives him an official place without forcing him into work he is not ready for. Seeing his classmates' effort clearly creates natural motivation to join in.
RG: Do not use it as a reward for dodging. If he is not working because of health or circumstances, respect that and be flexible. Do not force heavy work beyond his strength.
BT: Let him choose a light task within the activity, such as handing out water or filming memories of the class.
CN: I give you the post of labour referee; please note down the groups' efforts for me.

## The class is noisy while another class is sitting a test
LOI: The class next door is taking a test, yet your class is having a noisy, joking session. The invigilator next door has come over twice to remind you. The whole class still has not realised it is affecting others.
CHIEU: Class honoured for silence | Tell the class: I give you all a special mission: to protect the quiet of the next-door class for twenty minutes. Write on the board: Class guarding silence. When time is up, invite the next class to send a thank-you card. If your class keeps it, the card is stuck on your door. The whole class is a helper instead of the one being reminded.
THAY: I give the whole class a special mission: guard the quiet of the class next door, which is sitting a test, for twenty minutes. When we finish, we ask for a thank-you card to stick on our door.
TRO: (Whispering.) Yes, we can do it, sir.
THAY: I know. Keep your voices low, as if guarding a treasure.
VS: A class is noisy usually because it has not noticed the effect. Making silence a noble mission lets it see the role of helping others. The thank-you card is a moral reward.
RG: Do not punish the whole class for noise without a clear prior warning. If the neighbouring class really needs quiet, tell your class in advance. Do not use this move in place of sensibly arranging the schedule of activities.
BT: Post advance notice of the year group's tests so other classes can plan noisy activities elsewhere.
CN: Let us guard the quiet of the class next door for twenty minutes.
