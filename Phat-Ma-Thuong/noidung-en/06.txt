CHUONG: Swearing, nicknames, cruel jokes
EPI: Words are where young people first test their power. Teaching them to use words kindly is bigger than any subject.
LEAD: Adolescents swear to feel grown up, coin nicknames to feel funny, play cruel jokes to feel they belong. Punish heavily and they swear in secret, elsewhere. This chapter offers moves that help students see where their words land, and learn to speak differently while still being themselves.

## A student swears as a habit
LOI: Dat drops a swear word into nearly every sentence. He does it without thinking, aimed at no one. But the girls around him have begun to avoid him, and you hear it every time.
CHIEU: The substitute-word jar | Put a small jar on your desk. Each time Dat swears, you only look at him; he writes the word on a slip and drops it in the jar, with a substitute he invents himself, the funnier the better, such as good grief, oh my giddy aunt. At the end of the week you open the jar privately with him, pick the most inventive substitute and award a bonus point. The class sees only a jar, not what is inside.
THAY: Dat, I am not scolding you. Each time one slips out, write the word on a slip, drop it in the jar, and invent a really funny replacement. At week's end I pick the most creative one.
TRO: Um... I will try, sir.
THAY: Remember, the funnier the replacement, the better. I will wait for the weekend.
VS: Swearing is a reflex that needs another outlet. A creative substitute keeps the emotional release while making him practise changing words. Dropping the slip in the jar himself makes him observe himself.
RG: Do not read the jar's contents before the class. Do not use it when the swearing targets others, such as insulting family or appearance. Then switch to a serious conversation.
BT: Have the class compete to invent the best exclamation of the week and write the good ones on a corner of the board.
CN: Each time one slips out, write it in the jar and think of a really funny replacement.

## A student gives a classmate a cruel nickname
LOI: Minh is called by the whole class by a nickname about his weight. He laughs it off awkwardly, but you see his eyes darken. The first to use it was Hung, who still thinks it is fun.
CHIEU: A kind nickname | Meet Hung privately. Say: you have a gift for naming, I admire it. I challenge you: coin a nickname for Minh that speaks of his strengths. I will use it only if Minh agrees. He writes three ideas and you choose together. Then Hung asks Minh himself; if Minh nods, both use it. The old nickname dies a natural death.
THAY: Hung, you really have a gift for naming. I challenge you to think up a nickname for Minh about his strengths, three ideas. Then ask Minh whether he likes it.
TRO: Um... let me think about it, sir.
THAY: I will wait. A good nickname is one that the person called by it finds fun.
VS: A student who coins nicknames usually wants attention and to be funny. This keeps that talent and redirects it. Asking permission before using teaches respect for another's consent.
RG: Do not force Minh to forgive or take part. If Minh is deeply hurt, work with Minh first. Do not publicly punish Hung, to avoid drawing more attention to Minh.
BT: Open a real-names week in the class; anyone who uses a nickname gets a gentle reminder in the form of a wink.
CN: You have a talent for names. I challenge you to give a friend a nickname that friend will enjoy.

## A student hides a classmate's things as a prank
LOI: Several students hid Thu's bag as a prank; she searched the whole room, eyes reddening. The group laughed with delight. They returned it only when you asked, saying it was just a joke.
CHIEU: The search team owes a debt | Ask the group whether they know how many minutes Thu spent searching. Then give them a task: next morning each comes early and helps Thu with one thing, such as arranging books, carrying her bag, closing windows. Say: hiding her things cost Thu ten minutes of her life; you give it back with kindness. At the end of the day, Thu may tell how it felt, if she wishes.
THAY: By hiding the bag you made Thu lose ten minutes of searching and worrying. Tomorrow come early and each help Thu with one small thing, to give those ten minutes back.
TRO: Um... we are sorry, we did not think of it that way.
THAY: I know you meant no harm. But a joke that the other person does not laugh at is not a joke any more.
VS: Pranks tend to overlook the feelings of the one pranked. Giving the time back through concrete action helps them see the value of what they took, while saving their face.
RG: Do not make Thu accept anything. If you see signs of long-term bullying, it is no longer a joke and needs a firmer response with the school.
BT: Have the group write a shared apology, one sentence each, read privately to Thu.
CN: A joke the friend does not laugh at is not a joke. Tomorrow come early and help Thu for ten minutes.

## A student laughs at a classmate's wrong answer
LOI: Hoa raises her hand and answers wrongly. Someone laughs below and another says tsk. Hoa bows her head, and for the rest of the lesson never raises her hand again.
CHIEU: Thanking the one who dares to be wrong | Acknowledge on the spot: thank you, Hoa, for daring to answer; the wrong answer shows the class where the traps are. After the lesson, invite the student who laughed to write a two-line thank-you card to Hoa for helping the whole class understand, and leave it on her desk. Tell a story of the silliest mistake you ever made. After that the class laughs less, having seen you err too.
THAY: Thank you, Hoa, for daring to answer. That is exactly where many others would go wrong. Thanks to Hoa the whole class sees where to be careful.
TRO: (The student who laughed, at break.) Sir, I am sorry for laughing at Hoa.
THAY: Write Hoa two lines of thanks and leave them on her desk. I think Hoa will be glad.
VS: Laughing at a mistake makes the whole class reluctant to raise a hand. When you honour the one who dares to be wrong, the class norm changes: daring to err is admirable. The card turns the one who laughed into one who acknowledges.
RG: Do not make him apologise publicly, as it would embarrass Hoa further. Do not compare Hoa with a student who answered correctly.
BT: Open a feature, wrong answer of the week, and pick one wrong answer that helps the class understand more deeply.
CN: Thank you for daring to answer; this mistake helps the whole class understand better.

## A student jokes about a classmate's appearance
LOI: Son makes a remark about Trang's skin and his group laughs. Trang pretends not to hear but you see her lower her face, and afterwards she sits alone at break.
CHIEU: Three truly beautiful things | Meet Son privately, without blame. Say: jokes about appearance are remembered for a very long time. Your task: write three things that are truly beautiful about Trang that have nothing to do with looks, such as character, good habits, talents. Show me; if sincere, give it to Trang with a short apology in your own words. Next time he jokes about looks, you only hold up three fingers.
THAY: Son, I believe you meant no harm, but that remark could stay in Trang's heart a long time. Write three truly beautiful things about her, nothing about looks, and show me first.
TRO: Um... I can think of some, sir.
THAY: If it is sincere, give it to Trang with a short apology. If you cannot think of any yet, just tell me.
VS: A student who jokes about looks has seldom grasped the weight of words. Having to find real good in her forces him to see her as a whole person. Holding up three fingers is a quiet signal between you and him.
RG: Do not make him apologise before the class. Do not allow false praise; if he cannot find anything real, talk more rather than forcing him to write. If it is long-running bullying, respond more seriously.
BT: Have the whole class write a compliment card about something other than looks for a classmate drawn by lot, delivered via the class box.
CN: I believe you meant no harm. Write three truly lovely things about Trang, none about looks, and we will look at them together.
