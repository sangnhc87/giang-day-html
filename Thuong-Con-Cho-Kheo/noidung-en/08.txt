CHUONG: When your child is bullied or left out
EPI: A bullied child rarely says I am being bullied. They say my tummy hurts, I don't want to go to school.
LEAD: Most bullied children do not tell their parents. They fear being seen as weak, fear that parents will make a scene and bring revenge, or have come to believe they deserve it. Parents usually see only traces: a torn shirt, a missing pencil case, stomach aches every Monday morning. This chapter is about noticing, asking in a way that lets a child speak, and protecting them without making things worse.

## Your child does not want to go to school and has a stomach ache every morning
BC: For three weeks, every Monday morning, Bong, in grade 4, has clutched her tummy. The doctor finds nothing. A new pencil case has gone missing twice. On Sunday evenings she cries for no clear reason.
PX: Are you pretending so you can skip school? The doctor says nothing is wrong. Get up and go! | I say it hurts and they don't believe me. The real thing is even harder to say; they probably won't believe that either.
ME: (Saturday night, lying beside her.) Bong, I've noticed that for a few weeks your tummy has hurt on Monday mornings. I believe it really hurts.
CON: It really does, Mum.
ME: Yes. I read that sometimes the tummy hurts on behalf of the heart. When we are scared or sad about something we can't yet say, the tummy says it for us. Is anything at school scaring you?
CON: (Silent, turns to face the wall.)
ME: You don't have to tell me yet. I just want you to know: whatever happens at school is not your fault, and I will not scold you, and I won't do anything without asking you first.
CON: (Very quietly.) Thy and the others won't let me play with them. They hide my pencil case. They call me dirty.
ME: (Holds her.) Thank you for telling me. You have carried this alone for three weeks. You are very brave. You are not dirty, and you did nothing wrong.
CON: Don't tell the teacher. If they know I told, they'll hate me more.
ME: I understand what you're afraid of. I promise I won't do anything behind your back. Tomorrow we'll think together about how to tell the teacher in a way that keeps you safe. Tonight you sleep with me.
VS: In young children, prolonged stress very often becomes real physical symptoms: stomach aches, headaches, nausea, especially on school mornings. The child is not faking. Bullied children stay silent because of two fears: not being believed and being punished for telling. So the two promises that open the door are I believe you and I won't act without asking you. The first thing a child needs to hear after telling is it is not your fault, because the bully has convinced them of the opposite.
BH: When the tummy hurts and the doctor finds nothing, ask after the heart. And before asking, promise two things: I believe you, and I will do nothing behind your back.
LN: Check four signs over the past month: fear of school on particular days, belongings often lost or damaged, asking for extra money without a clear reason, changes in sleep and eating. With two or more, find a quiet moment to ask gently.
CN: Whatever happens at school is not your fault. I believe you.
TN: If my child were bullied, who would they tell first? Am I on that list?
TN: Have I ever brushed off my child's complaint of pain as laziness?

## Dad wants to teach his son to hit back
BC: Ti, in grade 6 and the smallest in his class, comes home with a button torn off. After much coaxing he says two older boys stopped him on the stairs, shoved him against the wall and took his breakfast money. The third time. Dad's face burns.
PX: What kind of boy is such a coward? If they hit you, hit back! Tomorrow if they touch you, punch them in the face; I'll answer for it! | I was beaten and now Dad calls me a coward. I daren't hit back, so I am both the victim and the one who shames him.
BA: (Holds himself back, sits down.) Three times and I'm only hearing now. Two bigger boys against you alone. Were you scared?
CON: Yes. I gave them the money to get it over with.
BA: One against two, both bigger: giving the money so you wouldn't be beaten was good judgement, not cowardice. Money can be earned again.
CON: You're not angry with me?
BA: I'm angry with them, not you. And angry that I'm only finding out now. Why didn't you tell me the first time?
CON: I was scared you'd say I'm weak. And they said if I told, they'd hit me harder.
BA: They threaten that because what they fear most is adults knowing. This is more than a grade 6 boy can handle alone, so now it's the adults' job. Tomorrow I'll see the deputy head and ask him to deal with it without naming you: there's a camera on the stairs, and there are supervisors.
CON: What if they still stop me?
BA: While the school acts, we have three moves. At break you always walk with at least one friend. Avoid that staircase; use the one near the supervisors' room. And if they stop you, shout very loudly: let go of me, then run towards an adult. Stand up and shout it for me now.
VS: Hit back sounds like teaching strength, but for a child outnumbered and outsized it is both dangerous and one more load of shame. Bullying is a deliberate imbalance of power, so children can rarely end it alone. Adults must step in. What parents can give are safety tactics that can be rehearsed: stay with a friend, avoid hidden spots, speak loudly and leave. Practising aloud at home helps the body remember what to do when frightened.
BH: Handing over money to avoid a beating is smart, not cowardly. Bullying is a job for adults, not a test of a child's courage.
LN: Practise three lines with your child, standing straight, eyes forward, voice loud: Stop. I don't like that. Let go of me. Three times each until the voice is steady. Then ask: which adult at school is easiest for you to reach?
CN: You are not a coward. This is too much for you alone, so now it is my job.
TN: Do I want my child to hit back because it is best for them, or because I feel ashamed?
TN: Do I know my child's school's procedure for bullying and whom to see first?

## Your child is shut out and mocked online
BC: Lately Quyen, in grade 9, turns pale whenever she picks up her phone. Mum happens to see the screen: a class group chat that excludes her, screenshots sent to her by someone. In it is an edited photo of her with mocking captions.
PX: Then stop looking; delete your accounts and it's over. And you must have done something for them to act like that. | Mum thinks it's my fault. And she will take my phone, so I am shut out and I lose the few friends I have left.
ME: Quyen, I accidentally saw your screen. I'm sorry I looked. But having seen it, I can't pretend I didn't. My heart hurts for you.
CON: (Bursts into tears.) They edit my photo into all kinds of things. The whole class has seen it. I don't want to go to school any more.
ME: (Holds her.) I'm here. Anyone ganged up on like that would crumble, not just you.
CON: Please don't take my phone.
ME: I won't. You've done nothing to be punished for. But I need your help with one hard thing: let's screenshot all of it and save it in one place, and then you never have to open it again. I'll keep it for you.
CON: Why screenshot it? I want to delete everything.
ME: I understand. But that is evidence. Editing photos to smear someone is serious; the school must deal with it, and they act properly when there's evidence. Don't reply to them at all, and don't ask friends to insult them back.
CON: I'm scared everyone will stare at me at school.
ME: I'll ask for a day off for you tomorrow and go to see your form teacher and the principal first. For tonight: is there anyone in the class you still trust?
CON: Van. She's the one who sent me the screenshots so I'd know.
ME: Then you still have one real friend. Invite Van over this weekend; I'll cook.
VS: Online bullying differs from bullying in person because it has no end of the school day: it follows the child into bed, has a large audience, and leaves lasting traces. Confiscating the phone is an understandable reflex, but to the child it is punishment for being a victim, and fear of losing the phone is exactly why many hide things from parents. The right order: stand with your child, save evidence, do not retaliate, report to the school and the platform, and help your child keep at least one real-life friendship.
BH: If your child is bullied online, do not take the phone. Save the evidence, do not hit back, report to those responsible, and keep one real friend close.
LN: Tell your child in advance, before anything happens: If anyone ever does something to you online, even if you did something wrong first, tell us. We will not take your phone away for telling the truth.
CN: You have done nothing to be punished for. I won't take your phone; I am on your side.
TN: If something happened online, would my child fear the bully more, or fear losing the phone more?
TN: Do I know which apps my child uses and which groups they are in?

## Your child has no friends
BC: For his twelfth birthday Duy invites nobody. At break he stays in the classroom reading comics. The teacher says he is well behaved and quiet. When Mum asks why he doesn't play with the others he says: I don't know what to say to them.
PX: You have to be more sociable. Who would play with someone so withdrawn? Go out and ask them to play; what's so hard about that! | Something is broken in me. Everyone finds it easy and only I find it hard. Mum is disappointed in me about this too.
ME: You said you don't know what to say to them. I understood at once, because I was exactly like that as a child. Standing in a group with no idea how to join in.
CON: You too? What did you do?
ME: I played with exactly one girl, Hanh, because we both collected stamps. We're still friends. I don't think you need the whole crowd either. You need one or two who suit you.
CON: The kids in my class only talk about football and shooting games. I don't like those.
ME: Then don't pretend to; that's exhausting. Let's go and find places with kids like you. You love chess and drawing comics. The district culture house has a chess class on Sunday mornings; want to come and watch one session? If you don't like it we go home.
CON: What if I can't talk to anyone there either?
ME: Then you play chess. Chess doesn't need much talking. After a game there's suddenly something to say: why did you move there? Friendship often starts with doing something together, not with talking.
CON: ...I'll try one session.
VS: Some children are introverted, prefer few friends and are perfectly fine. Others want friends, do not know how, and hurt because of it. The two look alike but need different responses, and only asking tells you which. Pushing a child to be sociable makes them feel defective. What helps is a small setting built around a shared interest, where friendship grows from doing things together. A single close friend is enough to shield a child from most of the harm of loneliness.
BH: Your child does not need to be liked by many. They need one or two real friends. Do not fix their nature; find them the right place.
LN: Ask your child: Is there anyone in class you feel comfortable sitting near? If so, create one small occasion: invite that child over to do one specific thing together, build a model, bake, play chess. One friend, one activity, two hours.
CN: You don't need the whole crowd. You only need one or two who suit you.
TN: Does my child have few friends and feel fine, or few friends and feel sad? Have I asked?
TN: Am I measuring my child with another family's ruler of lively and outgoing?
