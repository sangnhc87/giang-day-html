CHUONG: Lying, taking money, trying what they should not
EPI: The moment a child slips is the moment they look up to see whether you are reaching out a hand or raising one.
LEAD: Some things leave parents speechless: a bare-faced lie, money thinning in the wallet, a vape in the school bag, a child coming home smelling of beer. Instantly a very bad film of the future plays in our heads. But one slip is not a ruined life. This chapter discusses how to respond so that your child understands this is serious and also knows the road home is still open.

## Your child tells an obvious lie
BC: The cake Mum was saving in the fridge for Grandma has vanished. There is still cream at the corner of Bo's mouth; he is in grade 1. Mum asks who ate the cake and he answers at once: I don't know, maybe the cat.
PX: You're lying! I hate lying more than anything. There's cream all over your mouth. So little and already a liar! | Telling the truth gets me scolded for eating the cake. Lying and being caught gets me scolded worse. So next time wipe my mouth properly.
ME: (Kneels, wipes his mouth with a cloth.) I can see cream here. I think you ate the cake, and I think you were afraid I'd scold you, so you said it was the cat.
CON: (Looks down, nods.)
ME: Thank you for nodding. Do you know who I was saving that cake for?
CON: Grandma.
ME: Yes. Now Grandma has no cake, and I'm a little sad about that. But there's something more important than the cake: when you said the cat ate it, I didn't know which of your words to believe. I really want to believe you.
CON: I'm sorry, Mum. The cake was so yummy.
ME: It was a very good cake. Next time you want some, say Mum, I'd like some. I might say yes, I might say wait. But if you tell the truth I won't scold. I promise. Now, what shall we do for Grandma?
CON: I'll draw her a cake. And I'll use my piggy bank money to buy her another one.
ME: The drawing, yes, straight away. The buying, I'll pay half with you.
VS: Lying in small children is a normal developmental milestone: the child has just discovered that others cannot read their thoughts. At this age they lie mainly to avoid punishment, not from a dishonest character. Trapping a child with a question whose answer you already know is an invitation to lie. Stating plainly what you see, skipping the interrogation, showing that the truth is the gentler path, and letting the child repair the consequence builds honesty that lasts.
BH: If you already know the answer, do not ask in order to trap. Say what you see, and make telling the truth always more comfortable than lying.
LN: Next time you know for certain your child did something, replace who did this with a statement: I see milk spilled on the floor. Let's get a cloth. Watch whether your child denies less.
CN: If you tell the truth I won't scold. I promise, and I will keep my word.
TN: Do I often ask questions I already know the answer to, to test my child?
TN: Does my child hear me lie about small things: saying I'm not home when someone calls, promising and not doing?

## Money keeps disappearing from Mum's purse
BC: For the third time this month Mum counts her purse and finds it short: fifty thousand, a hundred, then two hundred. Only Lam, in grade 8, is in and out of the house. She has noticed he often has milk tea lately and new sandals that he says a friend gave him.
PX: I know you took it, don't deny it! Empty your bag! Once a thief, always a thief. This family has no thieves! | I have already been convicted. If I deny it I am stubborn; if I admit it I am a thief. This family has no place for someone like me.
BA: (Talks with Mum first, then speaks to his son alone at a cafe near home.) Lam, I have something hard to say. Mum's purse has been short three times this month. I am not searching your bag or setting a trap. I'll ask once, directly, and I will believe whatever you answer: did you take it?
CON: (A very long silence, turning his glass.) ...Yes. I took it.
BA: That was hard to say. I respect you for telling the truth. I'm not happy, but I respect it. Now tell me what you needed the money for.
CON: After school my friends go for milk tea. They all have money. If I don't go they call me stingy and poor. When I asked, Mum said you go to school, not out to play, so why do you need money.
BA: So you tried asking, were refused, and then took a shortcut. I understand the first part. The second is not acceptable: the money in Mum's purse is counted out for the market. She even suspected the part-time helper. An innocent person was nearly blamed because of you.
CON: (Startled.) I didn't know that.
BA: Now you do. I suggest three things. One, you tell Mum yourself, with me next to you. Two, you pay it back from pocket money. Three, our part: we have never given you regular pocket money, and that was a gap. From next week you get an amount each week; you manage it, and when it's gone it's gone.
CON: I get my own money?
BA: At your age you need to practise spending. Getting it wrong a few times while you are young and the sums are small is better than getting it wrong when you are grown.
VS: Children who take money at home usually have a real need with no legitimate way to meet it: fitting in with friends, wanting something they dare not ask for, sometimes being extorted or owing someone. Interrogation and searches teach a child to hide better. Asking once, directly, with a commitment to believe the answer, and then finding the need behind it, usually brings out the truth. Regular pocket money, even a little, is a good teaching tool: the child learns to plan and to wait, and loses the reason for shortcuts.
BH: Behind taken money there is always a need nobody heard. Deal with the taking seriously, then open a legitimate path for the need.
LN: If your child has no regular pocket money, start this week: a modest amount, given on the same day each week, with a clear agreement on what they pay for themselves. No advances, no top-ups when it runs out.
CN: I'll ask once, directly, and I will believe whatever you answer.
TN: When my child asks for money, do I hear out the reason or refuse at once?
TN: If my child were taking money because someone was forcing or threatening them, would the way I ask help them say so?

## There is a vape in your child's school bag
BC: Washing her son's bag, Mum finds a small device like a USB stick, smelling of mango. She looks it up: a disposable e-cigarette. Huy is in grade 9. Her hands shake.
PX: You smoke this? Fourteen and already an addict! Who got you into it? You're not leaving this house! | Mum sees me as an addict. Whatever I say she won't believe. Next time hide it somewhere else.
ME: (Waits for Dad; they agree on an approach. After dinner she puts the vape on the table.) Huy, I found this when washing your bag. Honestly, I'm frightened. I'm not shouting; I need to understand. Is it yours, or are you holding it for someone?
CON: ...It's mine. I bought it together with Phat.
ME: Thank you for not saying you were holding it for someone. How long have you been using it?
CON: About two months. At first just to try. We do it behind the canteen.
ME: Is it every day now, or once in a while?
CON: Nearly every day. On days without it I feel restless and irritable and can't study.
BA: That restlessness is nicotine. They make it mango or strawberry so we think it's harmless, but it contains nicotine, often stronger than cigarettes. Restless after two months means your body is already hooked. It's not that you're bad. You were caught by a product designed to be addictive.
CON: I want to quit too. I tried stopping for three days and couldn't stand it.
ME: You already tried to quit by yourself; I had no idea. Then we are on the same side. Quitting this alone is very hard; even adults fail. This week we'll take you to a doctor to ask how to do it properly. I'll keep this one. Do you have any others?
CON: One more in my desk drawer. I'll get it.
BA: Thank you. The first few weeks will be very uncomfortable. Whenever the craving is too strong, tell me, and we'll go and kick a ball, swim, anything.
VS: E-cigarettes target teenagers with flavours, easy-to-hide shapes and the rumour that they are harmless. A developing brain becomes addicted to nicotine faster than an adult's, sometimes within weeks. Once a child shows signs of dependence, restlessness and poor concentration without it, this is a health problem needing support, not a moral problem to be solved by punishment. Asking specific questions, how long and how much, gives parents the real picture. And naming the culprit as the addictive product puts the child on the parents' side against it.
BH: Against an addictive substance, parents and child must be on the same side. The enemy is nicotine, not the child.
LN: Spend ten minutes learning about e-cigarettes: what they look like, what teenagers call them, the harm. Then ask at dinner, in a curious voice: Does anyone at your school vape? What do you think about it? Listen before teaching.
CN: It's not that you are bad. Something designed to hook people caught you. We are on your side to get it off you.
TN: If my child were already caught up in something, would they trust that telling me brings help rather than a verdict?
TN: Does anyone at home smoke or drink in front of my child? What do they learn from that?

## Your child comes home from a birthday smelling of beer
BC: Eleven at night, an hour past the agreed time, Quang, in grade 11, gets home, face flushed, slightly unsteady, smelling of beer. He rode his own motorbike. His parents have been waiting in the living room; ten calls went unanswered.
PX: Seventeen and already drinking! Not answering your phone! Do you want to die? From now on you go nowhere! | My parents only know how to forbid. Next time, if I've been drinking, I'll stay at a friend's; don't come home, don't call.
BA: (Looks at him, voice low.) You're home. We've been waiting since ten. Now go shower, drink water and sleep. We'll talk in the morning. I don't talk to someone who has beer in him.
CON: I'm sorry... I...
ME: In the morning. Go to bed.
BA: (Next morning, all three at the table.) Now you're clear-headed. Let me say first what frightened me most last night: not that you drank. That you drank and then rode home. Ten calls with no answer; your mother was already thinking of hospitals.
CON: I'm sorry. They pushed me to drink; it's awkward not to. I only had three cans. I felt I could still ride.
BA: Everyone who has been drinking feels they can still ride. That is exactly what beer does to the head. I'm not asking whether you drink; I assume that at your age you'll try sooner or later. I need an agreement about staying alive: if you've had any, even one can, you don't ride, and you don't sit behind someone who has.
CON: Then how do I get home? If I call you I get shouted at.
ME: Here is our promise: you call, whatever the hour, and we come and get you. On the way home, not one question, not one scolding. Everything waits for the next morning. You only need to text one word, pickup.
CON: Really? No shouting?
BA: Not that night. We still talk the next day, and last night still has a consequence: coming home an hour late without telling us means no evenings out for two weeks. But we would rather collect a drunk child at two in the morning than receive a different phone call.
VS: The greatest danger to teenagers who drink is not the drink but what comes after: riding or driving, falls, being taken advantage of. The part of the brain that weighs risk is still maturing, and alcohol weakens it further. Talking to someone who is drunk is useless, so the first task is safety, and the conversation waits until morning. The no-questions pickup is a safety agreement many families use: it separates discipline from life and death, so a child never has to choose between a scolding and risking their life.
BH: Something matters more than teaching your child that very night: that they are alive in the morning to be taught. Give them a call that is always answered without a scolding.
LN: Agree on a rescue signal: your child only needs to text one word, such as pickup, or call and say a code phrase, and you come, asking nothing until the next day. It works for any situation where they feel unsafe.
CN: Any hour, text one word and we will come. On the way home, no questions and no scolding.
TN: If my child were stuck somewhere unsafe tonight, would they call me, or take the risk for fear of being shouted at?
TN: Can I keep my child's safety separate from discipline?
