CHUONG: Listening so your child will talk
EPI: Children do not stop talking because they run out of stories. They stop because each time they told one, they felt worse afterwards.
LEAD: Many parents complain: the older my child gets, the less they say; every answer is fine or nothing. Few recall that at five or six this same child talked all day long. That door did not close by itself. It closed a little each time the child shared something and got back a lecture, a criticism, or a nod from someone looking at a phone. The good news: a door that closed slowly can open slowly too.

## Every answer is fine
BC: Every afternoon at pick-up Mum asks the same question: how was school? Minh, in grade 8, answers flatly: fine. Then the earphones go in. If she asks more, he frowns: you keep asking.
PX: One simple question and you can't answer properly. We raise you and you treat us like strangers. | Mum is not asking because she wants to know how I am. She is checking, and I have just failed the check again.
ME: (Next day, no school questions. Hands him a milk tea.) I just picked this flavour. I don't know if you still like it.
CON: It's okay. Why did you buy it today?
ME: I was craving one and it felt odd to buy just one. By the way, something funny happened at work today. Want to hear?
CON: What?
ME: (Tells a short story that includes her own embarrassing moment.) ...I just froze. I still blush thinking about it.
CON: (Laughs.) Something just like that happened in my class today. Tin was asleep, the teacher called on him, and he stood up and answered for a different subject.
ME: Oh no, what did the teacher say?
CON: He laughed, everyone laughed. That teacher is easy-going. With the maths teacher he'd be finished...
VS: How was school sounds like caring, but to a teenager it smells of inspection, and the safest answer to an inspection is to say as little as possible. Teenagers open up through exchange: when an adult first shares something real, including a little clumsiness, the child feels invited into a conversation between equals. Sitting side by side in a car or sharing a snack is also easier than sitting face to face with eyes locked.
BH: If you want to hear your child's stories, try telling one of yours first. And do not grade the story they have just told.
LN: Change the question tonight. Instead of how was school, try one of these: Did anything make you laugh today? What did you do at break? Who was the kindest person you saw today? One question only, then listen.
CN: Something funny happened to me today. Want to hear?
TN: Of the ten things I say to my child each day, how many are questions, reminders and instructions, and how many are simply conversation?
TN: What does my child know about a day in my working life?

## Your child starts to tell and you start to teach
BC: Thao, in grade 6, comes home with a long face: her friend Ngan invited the whole group out to eat and left her out. Dad is washing dishes and has a solution before she finishes.
PX: You must have done something or they would have asked you. Take a look at yourself. And if they are like that, drop them and focus on studying. | If I tell, I am told I am wrong. Next time I'm sad, I'll keep it to myself.
BA: (Turns off the tap, dries his hands, turns around.) The whole group went and didn't ask you? That would hurt anyone.
CON: Yes. I saw the photos they posted. I was sitting right there and nobody said anything to me.
BA: You only found out from the photos. That hurts even more.
CON: I don't know what I did wrong. Yesterday Ngan and I were talking like normal.
BA: So you are sad and confused at the same time, not knowing what happened. Do you want me just to listen, or to think with you about what to do?
CON: Just listen. ...Maybe tomorrow I'll ask Ngan straight out.
BA: Okay. How will you ask?
CON: Gently. Something like, you guys went out the other day? Ask me next time.
VS: When a child shares something painful, the adult brain jumps straight into repair mode, because watching a child hurt is uncomfortable. But advice given too early carries a hidden message: your feeling is something to get rid of quickly. Naming the feeling accurately, hurt, confused, annoyed, visibly calms the emotion, and a calmer child often finds a solution alone, sometimes better than ours.
BH: A child does not share sadness to get a solution. They share so they do not have to be sad alone.
LN: Next time your child tells you something, practise three steps: stop what you are doing, repeat their point in your own words, then ask: Do you want me just to listen, or to give ideas? Do exactly what they choose.
CN: Do you want me just to listen, or to think it through with you?
TN: When did I last let my child finish a whole story without interrupting?
TN: When I am sad and someone rushes to advise me, how do I feel?

## Your child only opens up at eleven at night
BC: All day Han, in grade 10, says nothing. At eleven, when Mum is about to sleep, she drifts into the bedroom: Mum, are you asleep? Mum is exhausted and has an early meeting.
PX: Still awake at this hour? Whatever it is, tell me tomorrow. Go to bed. | My problem is not worth ten more minutes of Mum's time. By tomorrow I won't want to say it.
ME: Not yet. Come in. (Moves over, pats the empty space on the bed.)
CON: It's nothing... I was just asking.
ME: All right. Then lie here with me for a bit. It's been a while since we did that.
CON: (Silent for a long time.) Mum, did you ever feel like you weren't good at anything?
ME: Oh yes. In grade 11 I felt that all the time. Is that how you're feeling?
CON: Yes. Everyone in my class has something. One is good at English, one draws well. I have nothing.
ME: Lying alone with that thought is heavy. Tell me more; I'm not sleepy.
VS: Teenagers' body clocks run late, and darkness and quiet make them less self-conscious. So the truest things tend to surface at the most inconvenient hour, usually opened with a very casual test question. It's nothing is the child checking whether the door is really open. Fifteen minutes of lost sleep buys a message your child will keep for years: whenever I need them, my parents are there.
BH: Children do not pick a convenient time to open their hearts. They pick the moment they find the courage. That moment is rare; do not reschedule it.
LN: Tonight, before bed, stop by your child's room for two minutes. No homework check, no reminders. Sit on the edge of the bed and ask: was your day okay? Then say goodnight. Do it regularly, and on the day something happens, your child will know who to find.
CN: I'm not asleep yet. Come in.
TN: At what time of day does my child usually start a conversation with me? What am I usually doing then?
TN: What is my child's usual test question?

## Your child says something that shocks you
BC: At dinner, Phuc, in grade 9, drops a casual line: some kids in my class vape in the toilets. Mum's chopsticks stop. A dozen questions explode in her head at once.
PX: What?! Do you vape? Who are they? If you hang out with them you are in big trouble! Tomorrow I'm going to see your teacher! | Telling the truth gets me interrogated, and my friends will be in trouble because of me. From now on I know nothing.
ME: (Takes another piece of fish, keeps her voice normal.) Really. In the toilets?
CON: Yes. At break. It smells like fruit; teachers walk past and don't notice.
ME: Thank you for telling me. When you see that, what do you think?
CON: It seems normal. They say it's not as harmful as cigarettes.
ME: Yes, I've heard many people say that. What I have read is that it still contains nicotine, people get hooked within weeks, and lungs at your age are still growing. Has anyone offered you one?
CON: Once. I said I have asthma and that was that.
ME: That was a smart way to say no. Was it hard?
CON: A bit. They look at you like you're a coward.
ME: They looked at you like that and you still held your ground. I respect that. If it ever gets too hard to refuse, blame me: my mum checks everything. I'll happily be the villain.
VS: Children often raise sensitive topics by dropping a piece of information about someone else and watching how the parent reacts. It is a test. If the parent panics, the child concludes this kind of thing cannot be discussed at home, and bigger things later will never reach you. Keeping a normal face and asking what do you think tells you the most important thing: where your child stands in all this.
BH: How you react to today's small news decides whether you will hear tomorrow's big news.
LN: Practise one filler sentence for shocking news: Really? Tell me more. It buys you ten seconds to breathe and keeps your child talking.
CN: Thank you for telling me. What do you think about it?
TN: The last time my child told me something hard to hear, what did my face look like?
TN: Was there something at school I never dared tell my own parents? Why?
