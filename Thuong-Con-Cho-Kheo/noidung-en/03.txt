CHUONG: Praise, criticism and comparison: three double-edged knives
EPI: The right praise makes a child want to try harder. The wrong praise makes a child afraid of losing a title.
LEAD: Vietnamese parents traditionally love by criticising, fearing that praise will spoil a child. The next generation does the opposite and praises without limit: so good, so clever. Strangely, both can hold a child back. This chapter looks at three things parents use every day and rarely stop to think about: praise, criticism, and the phrase other people's children.

## Your child shows off a perfect score
BC: Khanh, in grade 4, bursts into the house waving a maths test with a bright red 10. His eyes shine, waiting for Dad to say something.
PX: Brilliant! My son is the smartest in the family! | I am loved because I am smart. So next time, if the problem is hard and I get it wrong, I am not smart any more. Better avoid hard problems.
BA: A ten! Let me see. (Turns the pages, looks closely.) This last one is long. How did you work it out?
CON: That one was really hard, Dad. I got it wrong once, crossed it out and did it again, see?
BA: I see the crossing out. You got it wrong and didn't give up; you worked through it again from the start. I like that even more than the ten.
CON: Because last week I did this kind of exercise three times.
BA: Ah, so this mark didn't fall from the sky. It came from those three practice sessions. How do you feel inside?
CON: I feel great!
BA: Remember that feeling. It is yours; you earned it yourself.
VS: Research on praise points to something worth pondering: children praised for being smart tend to choose easier tasks next time to protect the label, and give up sooner when things get hard. Children praised for their method, their effort, their willingness to redo tend to choose harder tasks and last longer when they stumble. The reason is simple: smartness is not something a child can control; effort is.
BH: Praise what your child can control: effort, method, persistence. Do not praise what they can only fear losing: smart, the best, number one.
LN: Today, find one thing your child did, however small, and praise it with this formula: I noticed you (specific action), and because of that (result). For example: I noticed you packed your bag last night, so this morning nobody had to rush.
CN: I saw you get it wrong and then sit down to do it again. I value that more than the ten.
TN: Do I tend to praise who my child is (clever, good, smart) or what my child does?
TN: Is my child avoiding hard things for fear of not being good at them?

## The drawing is not good and your child asks if it is beautiful
BC: Su, in grade 2, has been drawing all afternoon and brings Mum a picture Mum cannot make out at all. She asks: is it pretty, Mum?
PX: Very pretty, very pretty. (Eyes still on the phone.) | Mum is not looking. Her praise is just noise. From now on I can't be sure anything she praises is true.
ME: (Puts the phone down, picks up the picture.) Oh, so many colours. Tell me about this one.
CON: This is a dragon. It's flying over a rainbow to get to its home in the clouds.
ME: Ah, here are the wings. I like how you coloured the tail from red to orange; it looks like it's on fire.
CON: Because it breathes fire. I coloured three layers.
ME: Three layers, no wonder it's so bold. Did this take you long?
CON: Very long. But the head doesn't look right yet.
ME: Which part doesn't look right to you?
CON: The mouth. Tomorrow I'll look at a dragon picture and draw it again.
VS: Children need to be truly seen more than they need to be called good. A vague compliment delivered with eyes elsewhere is recognised immediately, and over time the child stops trusting the parent's praise. Describing exactly what you see and asking about it is the most honest praise there is, without a single lie. The child even points out what she is not satisfied with, which means she is learning to evaluate herself, far more valuable than waiting for someone else's verdict.
BH: You do not need to lie and say it is beautiful. Look closely, say what you see, and ask your child about it.
LN: Next time your child shows you something, do not say pretty or good. Name one specific detail you notice, then ask: Which part do you like best?
CN: Tell me about this one. Which part do you like best?
TN: Do I often praise just to finish the moment so my child will go away?
TN: Does my child comment on their own work, or always wait for an adult's verdict?

## Other people's children
BC: Mum comes back from the parents' meeting with the class ranking. Mrs Lan's son from the same row of rented rooms is third; her own son is twenty-eighth. Seeing Tuan, grade 8, lying there with his phone, she feels it rise in her throat.
PX: Look at Huy. His family is poorer than ours and he's third in class. All you are good at is eating and playing. | Mum wishes Huy were her son. I am her disappointment. So why bother trying.
ME: (Puts the paper on the table, sits next to him.) I'm back from the meeting. Honestly, I'm worried, but I don't want to scold. I want to understand.
CON: I know. Twenty-eighth. Are you going to compare me with Huy again?
ME: I've done that for years, I know. And after all my comparing you did not study any better; we only grew further apart. So today I'm not comparing. I'll only compare you with you: last term you got 5 in maths, this term 6.5. What did you do differently?
CON: ...I sit near Dat. He explained some geometry problems to me.
ME: So when someone explains it again, it goes in. Which subject feels hardest right now?
CON: English. I've been lost since grade 6. In class I don't understand anything the teacher says.
ME: Sitting through a whole lesson understanding nothing must be awful. I didn't know that. Let's work out where to start untangling it.
VS: Parents compare hoping the child will look at the example and try. But a compared child does not see an example; they see themselves ranked below another child in their parent's heart. The usual reaction is to hate the other child, or to give up so as not to run a race they are sure to lose. Comparing a child with who they were last month is entirely different: that race can be won, and every small step forward is seen.
BH: Compare your child with another and they learn to dislike the friend and themselves. Compare them with who they were yesterday and they learn to move forward.
LN: Tonight, find one thing your child has improved in compared with six months ago, whatever it is: getting up alone, arguing less, half a mark higher, helping cook. Tell them specifically, with no but.
CN: I am not comparing you with anyone. I am only looking at you today and you before.
TN: Who was I compared with as a child? What do I feel now when I think of that person?
TN: If my child compared me with their friends' parents, how would I feel?

## When you must criticise, do it so they still want to improve
BC: Tram, in grade 7, is on dinner duty. The rice is mushy, the soup salty, the kitchen looks like a battlefield, eggshells still in the sink. Dad comes home hungry and looks at the kitchen.
PX: What kind of daughter can't even cook a meal properly? Who is going to clean up this mess? | I am clumsy and useless. I tried and still got criticised. Next time I won't offer to cook, so I won't be scolded.
BA: (Eats a bowl first.) Did you cook all of this alone today? This fried egg is just right; I like it.
CON: Yes. But the rice came out mushy and I slipped with the soup.
BA: True, the rice is a bit soft and the soup a bit salty; I can taste it. You noticed straight away, which is good. Where do you think it went wrong?
CON: The rice, I think I measured water the way we do for the old rice. And the soup, I seasoned it and didn't taste.
BA: Then you already have two lessons without my saying anything. There is one thing I do need to say directly: the kitchen. Eggshells in the sink, pan unwashed. Cooking is not finished while the kitchen looks like this.
CON: I was so tired after cooking.
BA: I understand; cooking alone really is tiring. Next time try cleaning as you cook: wash the chopping board while the water boils. After we eat, I'll wash the dishes and you wipe the counters; ten minutes and it's clean.
VS: Criticism aimed at the person, clumsy, lazy, bad, leaves no path to improvement, because a child cannot stop being themselves. Feedback aimed at one specific task has a clear way out. Order matters too: acknowledge what worked, let the child name what did not, and only then add what they missed. A child who names their own mistake fixes it faster than one who has it pointed out.
BH: Criticise the work, not the person. And let your child name what went wrong before you do.
LN: Next time you need to give feedback, begin with a question: What do you think went well, and what would you do differently next time? Listen to it all, then add exactly one thing you think matters most. Only one.
CN: This job isn't right yet, and we can fix it. I am not criticising you.
TN: Which criticism from my own parents do I still remember word for word?
TN: How do I give feedback to a colleague? Is it very different from how I speak to my child?
