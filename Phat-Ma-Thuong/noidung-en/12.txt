CHUONG: Uniform, hair, litter and snacking
EPI: These faults are small, but small and repeated wear a teacher out more than big ones. Punish with kindness, not with nagging.
LEAD: Shirts untucked, dyed hair, littering, snacking in class are routine faults you remind about every day. Nag and it goes stale, punish heavily and it is excessive. This chapter offers light moves, like role changes and short challenges, that lead the student to feel the real cost of small faults. Most important is telling apart the student who errs deliberately from the one who errs for lack of means.

## A student does not wear the uniform
LOI: Huy has come to school for several days running without uniform, in an ordinary T-shirt. He gives no explanation, only says he forgot. The homeroom teacher has reminded him, and friends have noticed.
CHIEU: Ask first, shirt after | Meet him privately and ask gently: lately you often wear ordinary clothes; is something making it hard to wear the school shirt? If he says it is torn, too small, or the family has not had time to buy one, you discreetly hand him a shirt from the class spare wardrobe or connect him with a support fund. If he simply does not like it, you negotiate: wear the uniform during the week, and at the weekend he picks a special day suggested by the class.
THAY: Huy, lately I see you often wear ordinary clothes. Is there something making it hard to wear the school shirt? I ask to help, not to punish.
TRO: Um... my shirt has got too small and we have not been able to buy one.
THAY: Thank you for telling the truth. I have a way to help; do not feel shy.
VS: Wearing the wrong uniform may be disobedience, but often it is a matter of circumstances. Asking first avoids punishing unfairly and gives you a chance to help discreetly. He keeps his self-respect.
RG: Do not reveal his circumstances. Do not punish him because his family is poor. When he is merely defying the rule, speak frankly but still privately.
BT: Set up a spare uniform closet from donations of old uniforms from previous years.
CN: Lately I see you in ordinary clothes. Is there something I can help with?

## A student dyes her hair and paints her nails
LOI: At the start of the week, Nhi comes to school with bleached blond hair and glossy nails. The rules do not allow it. The whole class looks at her curiously and she holds her head high.
CHIEU: A week of challenge | Invite Nhi to stay and say gently: your hair stands out, really. But the school has a rule, and I need you to understand the reason. Propose a week of challenge: she gives you three reasons why she chose that hairstyle, and you tell her the reasons behind the rule. Together see which is more convincing. She may suggest to the class committee that the rule be reconsidered, or choose to dye at a time like the summer holiday.
THAY: Nhi, your hair colour does stand out. I would like to hear why you chose it, and then I will tell you the reasons for the rule. Let us see whether talking helps.
TRO: Um... I just wanted to look a little different, sir.
THAY: I understand. Wanting to be different is normal. Let us find a way for you to stay yourself and still follow the rules.
VS: Dyeing hair is how adolescents assert themselves. You listen first and then explain, so she feels respected and cooperates more easily. If the rule itself has problems, the feedback channel is a civics lesson.
RG: Do not humiliate her appearance. Do not cut her hair or make her wash it out at once. You must follow school rules and notify parents where regulations require. If the dye is for a special reason, such as illness, be flexible.
BT: Invite her to be the class representative giving feedback to the headteacher about the hair and grooming rules.
CN: I would like to hear why you chose this colour, and then tell you the reasons for the rule.

## A student litters
LOI: At break, floors and yard are full of snack wrappers and bottles. One student has just finished eating and dropped it on the ground and walked away; the whole group saw it and no one picked it up.
CHIEU: Pick up three | Do not punish any one student. The whole class or the group that littered gets a short challenge: in the next break, pick up three pieces of litter that are not theirs and photograph them for you. Add one question: how did it feel to pick up someone else's litter? After picking up, they get a green-leaf sticker in their exercise book. Picking up other people's litter teaches the feeling of being put out, instead of only hearing about it.
THAY: A small challenge for you: next break pick up three pieces of litter that are not yours and photograph them for me. Afterwards tell me how it felt.
TRO: Um... I will do it. A bit tiring, sir.
THAY: Yes, tiring, right? That is how the cleaner feels every day. Thank you.
VS: Students litter because they have not seen the consequences for others. Picking up others' litter gives direct experience. The green leaf turns duty into recognition.
RG: Do not make them pick up dangerous things like needles or broken glass. Do not make a student do it alone in a deserted spot. Do not use litter picking as a routine punishment.
BT: Hold a clean-class week; the cleanest class by Friday picks the song played at break.
CN: Please pick up three pieces of litter that are not yours, then tell me how it felt.

## A student snacks in class
LOI: In the middle of a lesson, the smell of crisps drifts from the back. A student sneaks bites behind his notebook. You remind him and he says yes; a while later he is eating again. Those beside him begin to whisper.
CHIEU: A five-minute feast | Smile and say: you are hungry, really? I give you a five-minute feast right now; step out to the corridor and eat quickly, nobody sees. When he comes back say: just now was priority for the stomach, now it is priority for the head. Before lessons from now on, he writes on a sticky note whether he has eaten so you know. If a student is truly hungry, you check on him quietly.
THAY: You are hungry, right? I give you five minutes to eat in the corridor, then come back. Once the stomach is done, the head belongs to me.
TRO: Um... I am sorry, sir. I did not have time for breakfast.
THAY: That is fine. Thank you for telling me honestly. Tomorrow eat before class, or I will help you find a way.
VS: Snacking in class is usually hunger or habit. Letting him eat in a controlled way answers the real need and drops the confrontation. He understands that the rule is not there to make things hard but to keep the class in rhythm.
RG: If he skips meals because the family is struggling, do not punish; give practical support, such as contacting school meals or a support fund. Do not publicise that he is hungry. Do not allow regular eating in lessons.
BT: A general rule of three minutes for a light snack mid-lesson if the whole class is truly hungry in the morning.
CN: If you are hungry I give you five minutes to eat outside and then we study.
