CHUONG: Talking back, slammed doors, you just don't understand
EPI: The child who pushes parents furthest away is often checking whether they are still standing there.
LEAD: The child who yesterday begged to sleep in your bed now locks the door and answers with a sigh. Parents feel shut out, and that hurt easily turns into anger about rudeness. But separating from parents is something adolescents must do in order to become people. This chapter is about holding a boundary on respect without stifling growth, and about staying close while your child keeps pushing you off.

## Your child argues back, word for word
BC: Mum reminds Tri, in grade 8, to tidy his room. He answers: it's my room; how I keep it is my business. Mum says: this is my house. He snaps: then throw me out on the street! Both voices rise.
PX: Is that how you speak to your mother? Rude! I gave birth to you and raised you, and now you answer back! | Mum drags out giving birth to me in order to win. Whatever I say is rude. So I won't speak.
ME: (Stops. Drops her voice much lower.) Wait. We are shouting at each other. I don't want to talk like this. I'll stop first.
CON: (Still glaring.) Because you keep forcing me.
ME: I hear you say I force you. In ten minutes, when we've both cooled down, I want to hear all of it. I'm going to get some water.
ME: (Ten minutes later, knocks.) May I come in? Go ahead, I'm listening. Where am I forcing you?
CON: You remind me about everything. It's my room and you come in and tidy and throw my things away. The other day you threw out the box I was saving for a model.
ME: I thought that box was rubbish. I threw it out without asking; that was wrong of me. So you're upset because I touched your things, not really because of the tidying.
CON: Yes. I'm grown now and you act like I'm a little kid.
ME: I see. Then let's divide it again: your room is yours; I won't come in to tidy or throw anything away. In return there are two things that affect the whole house, so I need them: no food or dishes left in the room overnight, and dirty clothes in the basket. The rest is up to you.
CON: That's okay then.
ME: One more thing. You are allowed to disagree with me and to say you're annoyed. But throw me out on the street hurt me. Next time you're annoyed, say I'm annoyed because you did this and this, and I'll listen.
CON: ...Sorry for that. I just said it.
VS: In an argument, one voice rises and the other follows, and beyond a certain point nobody hears anybody. The adult is the one able to brake first. Lowering your voice and pausing is not losing; it is modelling exactly the skill you want your child to have. Behind a teenager's back-talk there is usually a legitimate request clumsily expressed: I want my own space, I want to be treated as grown. Meeting the reasonable part of that request and keeping the boundary on how we speak can be done side by side.
BH: Your child has the right to disagree with you. They do not have the right to insult you. Neither do you. Whoever is calmer brakes first.
LN: With your child, choose three non-negotiables at home (for safety and for other people), and leave the rest to them within their own space. Write them down. The fewer the rules, the easier they are to keep.
CN: We are shouting at each other. I'll stop first. In ten minutes I want to hear all of it.
TN: Of ten reminders I give my child a day, how many truly matter?
TN: When I argue with my child, do I want to win or to understand?

## Your child slams the door and locks it
BC: After a few remarks from Dad about marks at dinner, An, in grade 9, drops her chopsticks, walks to her room, slams the door and locks it. Dad stands outside, hand already on the handle.
PX: Open this door! I'm counting to three! Nobody in this house slams a door in a parent's face. Tomorrow I'm taking this door off! | I have nowhere to hide. Dad comes in when he wants, removes the door when he wants. Nothing is mine.
BA: (Lets go of the handle. Stands quietly, then speaks through the door in a normal voice.) An, I'm not coming in. What I said earlier was harsh, and in the middle of dinner; I know. If you need to be alone, be alone.
CON: (Silence.)
BA: I've left your dinner on the table, covered. Whenever you want to talk, I'm in the living room. If not tonight, that's fine too.
CON: (An hour later, opens the door a little and comes out for the food. Dad says nothing; he just reheats the soup.)
CON: (Halfway through her bowl.) You said it in front of everyone, even Teo. He laughed at me.
BA: Yes. Your marks are your business and I brought them up in front of your brother. I was wrong there. Next time I'll speak to you in private. And about the door: you have the right to close your door when you need to be alone. I only ask you not to slam it; it startles everyone, and Grandma has a weak heart.
CON: Okay. I was just so angry.
BA: When you're angry, go to your room and close the door gently. And one more request: don't lock it. I promise never to open it myself; I will always knock and wait for your answer. But in case something happens, I need to be able to get in.
VS: For teenagers, a closed room is the only place where they can settle their feelings unobserved. Going to one's room when angry is in fact a fairly healthy response: the child is removing themselves instead of saying something worse. Breaking in or threatening to remove the door takes away that last retreat. Respecting the closed door, saying clearly that you are outside and available, and letting the child come out at their own pace usually shortens the anger more than any order.
BH: A closed door is not a declaration of war. It is a child needing somewhere to calm down. Knock, wait, and stay near.
LN: From today, knock on your child's door and wait for an answer before entering, every time. This small habit tells your child they are respected, and they will learn to knock on yours.
CN: If you need to be alone, be alone. I'm in the living room; whenever you want to talk, I'll listen.
TN: Is there anywhere at home that truly belongs to my child?
TN: When I am angry, how long do I need alone? Do I give my child the same?

## Your child says I hate you
BC: Mum will not let Ly, in grade 5, sleep over at a friend's because she does not know the family. Ly cries and screams: I hate you! You're the worst mum! I wish I were in another family! The last line goes in like a knife.
PX: Is that so? Then go and find another mother! I sacrificed my whole life for you to hear this! | I said one thing in anger and Mum really wants to get rid of me. Her love can be taken back.
ME: (Breathes in. It hurts, but she does not answer with her hurt.) You are very angry with me. You badly wanted to go and I said no.
CON: You always say no! Ha's family lets her go everywhere!
ME: It feels unfair to you. I hear that. Be angry with me for as long as you need. I am still not changing the decision, because I don't yet know who Thu's parents are, and my job is to keep you safe. And even if you hate me, I love you exactly the same.
CON: (Cries louder, runs to her room.)
ME: (Half an hour later, sits on the edge of her bed.) Have you finished crying? I know you're very sad.
CON: (Sniffling.) I don't really hate you.
ME: I know. When we're very angry we reach for the heaviest words we can find. Next time, try I am so angry with you. It's more accurate, and it hurts me less.
CON: Are you sad?
ME: Yes, a little. But I don't stop loving you because of a sentence. About the sleepover, here's an idea: invite Thu to sleep at our house this weekend, or you go and play there until eight and I pick you up, so I can meet her mum first. Which do you choose?
VS: A child's I hate you is an emotion too large spilling through a still small vocabulary. A child only dares say it to someone they are sure will not leave. If the parent answers with hurt or a threat of abandonment, the child learns that their strong feelings are dangerous enough to break love. Holding the decision, naming the anger, and affirming unchanged love teaches two things at once: the limit is firm, and Mum is steady.
BH: I hate you means I am very angry and I trust you are strong enough to hear it. Hold the decision. Hold your arms open too.
LN: Teach your child a set of words for anger in place of hurtful ones: I'm angry, I'm upset, it feels unfair, I need to be alone. Write them on the fridge. Use exactly those words yourself when you are angry.
CN: Even if you are angry with me, even if you hate me, I love you exactly the same. And I am still not changing the decision.
TN: When my child says something that hurts, do I respond as a steady adult or as a wounded person who wants to strike back?
TN: Have I ever threatened to leave my child, give them away, or stop loving them?

## Your child says you two understand nothing
BC: Binh, in grade 11, goes straight to his room these days. Whatever is asked: you wouldn't understand. Dad tries to open a conversation by describing how hard school was in his day; Binh puts his earphones in.
PX: We've eaten more salt than you've eaten rice. There's nothing we haven't been through. You think you have it hard? In my day I had to... | Dad has turned my story into his again. My hardship doesn't count because the old days were harder.
BA: (Knocks, stands in the doorway.) Binh, one question and then I'll go. You say we don't understand. I've thought about it and you're right. I really don't.
CON: (Takes out one earphone, surprised.) ...Sorry?
BA: I grew up without the internet or group chats, and exams were different. I keep applying the old days, but your times are different. So of course I don't understand. I want to, but you'll have to show me.
CON: Show you what?
BA: What a day of yours is like. What tires you most. I promise to listen without saying in my day and without advice, unless you ask.
CON: (After a pause.) The most tiring thing is always having to be okay. Okay in class, cheerful online, well behaved at home. I don't know when I'm allowed not to be okay.
BA: ...I had never thought of that. That sounds heavy. In this house you are allowed not to be okay. I mean it.
CON: You say that, but if I look down Mum asks me a dozen questions.
BA: True, because when we see you sad we panic. So let's agree: on a day when you're not okay and don't want to talk, just say I need quiet. We'll leave you in peace and only bring food. All right?
CON: ...All right. Thanks, Dad.
VS: You wouldn't understand is both a reproach and an invitation left open. Answering with how much harder things were in the past declines the invitation, because it compares and takes the leading role. Children's world today really is different: the pressure to be present and fine online around the clock is something earlier generations never carried. Admitting you do not understand does not make you smaller. It turns you from a judge into a learner, and teenagers are very willing to teach an adult who is willing to learn.
BH: When your child says you don't understand, the answer that opens the door is: you're right, I don't yet; show me.
LN: Ask your child to show you one thing from their world: a song they are listening to, a channel they follow, a game they play. Listen or watch to the end and ask why they like it. No criticism.
CN: You're right, I don't understand yet. I want to; show me.
TN: How many times a week do I begin a sentence with in my day?
TN: What does my child's world contain that my generation never had to face?
