CHUONG: Clashes and dangerous behaviour: fighting, bullying, tobacco, prohibited items
EPI: These are no longer jokes. The best move now is the one that keeps people safe first, and only afterwards speaks of punishment.
LEAD: This chapter differs from the others. When there is violence, prolonged bullying, addictive substances or dangerous objects, being fun and gentle is no longer the first aim. The first aim is safety. But within that safety, teachers still have many ways to make the handling teach and keep the relationship with the student, instead of merely punishing. Each situation below comes with the reporting and coordination that teachers should not skip.

## Two students scuffle and fight
LOI: At break, Long and Hai argue, then shove and punch each other. Friends pull them apart. Both are panting, faces red, eyes blazing. One has a bleeding nose.
CHIEU: Two accounts | First separate them, check injuries, take the injured one to the infirmary. After ten minutes of cooling, ask each to sit in a separate corner and write his own account: how it began, what he felt, what he did. Read each, then let them swap and read each other's, with you beside them. The aim is not to decide who was right, but for each to see events through the other's eyes. Then ask: what do you two need so this does not happen again?
THAY: You two sit apart and each write down what just happened as you saw it. I will read both. Whoever is hurt, I take to the infirmary first.
TRO: (After swapping.) Um... I did not know he thought that.
THAY: Thank you for reading. Now let us talk about what you two need so it does not repeat.
VS: Fights happen when feeling outruns words. Writing cools the feeling and separates the matter from the anger. Reading each other's account lets each see the half of the truth he has missed, and is the first step towards the two negotiating by themselves.
RG: Physical safety and first aid come first. Report to the school, and inform families as regulations require, especially where there are injuries. Do not make the two shake hands while still angry. If one had been bullied for long before hitting back, treat it as bullying; do not punish them equally.
BT: If both agree, a few days later give a short cooperative game with you as referee, such as building a model together.
CN: You two sit apart and write what just happened as you saw it. I take the hurt one to the infirmary first.

## A student is bullied over a long period
LOI: Tin is called by a nickname by a group, has his bag hidden, receives insulting messages in the class chat, and is gradually isolated. He says nothing but is losing weight and often asks for leave. Another student quietly told you.
CHIEU: The class circle and the repair plan | Do two things in parallel, discreetly. With Tin: meet privately and say plainly that you know, it is not his fault, and you will deal with it. Give him a teacher he can find at any time. With the bullying group: meet each privately; do not let the group confront each other. Say straight that this behaviour is unacceptable, and each draws up a three-step repair plan: stop, apologise by a letter without pressing the victim to forgive, and do one kind thing for the class. Also call both families and tell the school. In class, form a friendly circle round Tin without labelling him a pitiable case.
THAY: (To Tin.) I know what has been happening. It is not your fault. I will deal with it, and you can find me at any time, even outside lessons.
TRO: (Tin, choking.) Um... thank you, sir. I was afraid that if I told, they would hate me more.
THAY: I will make sure nobody knows it was you who told. You were very brave to bear it so long.
VS: Victims of bullying are often silent for fear of revenge. First he must feel safe and blameless. Handling the group must be clear and systematic, since repeated behaviour must have real consequences, but the repair plan gives them a way to change.
RG: Protecting the victim is priority one. You must report to the school and family, and keep evidence such as messages. Do not force face-to-face reconciliation before the victim is ready. Do not make the victim apologise or yield. If there are signs of self-harm, tell family and a counsellor at once.
BT: Invite a small group of kind classmates to accompany Tin for a week, such as eating lunch together, without letting him feel pitied.
CN: I know, and it is not your fault. I will deal with it, and you can find me at any time.

## A student smokes or vapes
LOI: The caretaker reports a smell of smoke in the toilets behind the school. You go and find Hung with two friends holding a vape. All three panic, hiding their hands behind their backs.
CHIEU: Twenty-four hours smoke-free and a research task | Take the vape safely, record it, do not shout. Say: I am more worried about your health than angry. Give two tasks: one, twenty-four hours without using it, noting how you feel and when you crave it; two, each finds one real fact about the harm of vaping to people your age and tells you in five minutes. Inform the family, with information on help for minors to quit. Do not laugh or threaten, only ask questions.
THAY: I am keeping this device safely. I am more worried about your health than angry. Two tasks for you: twenty-four hours without it, noting when you crave it most, and find one real fact about the harm to tell me.
TRO: Um... we are sorry. We thought it was okay, sir.
THAY: Many people think so, which is why I want you to read for yourselves. If it is hard, come to me and I will help find support.
VS: Threats rarely work on tobacco, because students are used to hearing threats. Noting cravings and finding evidence themselves lets them see the danger in information they chose. Informing the family ensures support outside school.
RG: You must inform family and school as regulations require. Do not search phones or bags excessively. Do not sneer or say you knew they would turn out bad. If the student is badly dependent, advise the family to take him to a medical facility or youth cessation counselling.
BT: If many use it, invite a health professional to talk to the whole year with real figures and images, not threats.
CN: I am more worried about your health than angry. Twenty-four hours without it, then we talk.

## A student brings a prohibited item: knife, firecracker, lighter
LOI: In the middle of the lesson, a classmate whispers to you: Kiet has a small knife in his bag. You look at Kiet; he bows his head. He says it is only for sharpening pencils, but it is a flick knife.
CHIEU: The safe-keeping box | Stay perfectly calm. Invite him outside, speak privately, and ask him to hand the knife over himself, placing it in a lidded box you have ready. Record it with him, without scolding. Say: I believe you have no intention of harming anyone, but a knife in school is something we cannot allow. Ask: why did you bring it? Has someone threatened you, or did you feel you needed to protect yourself? Tell the school and invite the family to collect it, and hear his real reason together.
THAY: Kiet, hand me the knife and put it in this box. I am not shouting at you. I only want to ask: why did you bring it? Is someone frightening you?
TRO: (Silent a moment.) Um... some older boys at the gate keep stopping me, I am scared.
THAY: Thank you for telling me. I will deal with this with the school; you do not have to cope alone.
VS: Many students carry prohibited items out of fear, not malice. Staying calm helps him hand it over honestly, and asking the reason lets teachers protect him where it is truly dangerous. The ritual of handing it over in a box keeps his dignity.
RG: The safety of the whole class is the priority. You must report to the school, family, and as regulations require. Do not wrestle with him or search him alone. If the item is a dangerous weapon or there is a threat, tell the headteacher and authorities at once through proper procedure.
BT: With the school, set up an anonymous reporting line for students when someone brings something dangerous, so no student has to accuse directly.
CN: Hand me this and I will not shout. I want to know why you brought it, and whether someone is frightening you.

## A student threatens: I will deal with you outside
LOI: After a quarrel, Dat points a finger at a classmate and says: when we get out of the school gate you are dead. The whole class hears. The other student turns pale. Dat is still furious.
CHIEU: A meeting in the teacher's office | Invite Dat outside, saying nothing before the class. Say calmly: I take what you just said very seriously. I do not want anyone hurt, neither the other student nor you. Let him sit quietly for ten minutes, drink water, then say: at the end of school I will walk with both of you to the gate and you go in different directions. Tomorrow morning, if both agree, I invite you two to sit and talk. Tell the school and both families.
THAY: Dat, I heard what you said and I take it very seriously. I do not want you or him hurt. Sit quietly for ten minutes, drink some water, and then we will talk.
TRO: Um... I only said it, I would not really do it, sir.
THAY: Perhaps so, but I cannot take the risk. This afternoon I will walk you both to the gate, each in a different direction.
VS: A threat often comes from anger, but both students need protection. Staying calm and serious shows him you do not take it lightly, nor do you shame him. Walking them to the gate prevents harm happening immediately.
RG: Treat a threat as serious until you are sure it is not. You must report to the school and the families. Do not leave the two alone to face each other while still angry. If there are signs he was abused and is imitating, he needs psychological support.
BT: Teach the whole class a short technique for anger: count down from ten, step away, tell an adult.
CN: I take what you said very seriously. I do not want anyone hurt, and I will walk you both to the gate.
