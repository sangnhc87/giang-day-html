CHUONG: Laziness, procrastination, lost foundations: do not rush to call it lazy
EPI: Lazy is a label adults stick on something they have not yet understood.
LEAD: Truly lazy children are rare. The one sprawled out refusing to sit at the desk may be afraid of a subject they no longer understand. The one who leaves everything to the last minute may not know where to start. The one who says school is boring may be exhausted. The word lazy throws all of these into one basket and prescribes the same medicine: scolding. This chapter opens the basket and looks at what is inside.

## Ten reminders and still not at the desk
BC: Seven in the evening. Mum's first reminder: go and study. Eight o'clock, the fourth. Bao, in grade 5, is still on the sofa: yes, in a bit. At nine Mum cannot take any more.
PX: How many times do I have to tell you? Lazy as anything! Study time comes and you act as if you're being sent to prison! | Mum only gets angry at the tenth reminder, so the first nine don't count. And I am a lazy kid; that is just what I am.
ME: (Next day, five in the afternoon, when both are in a good mood.) Bao, I want to discuss something. Every evening I remind you, you get annoyed, and I get tired. I don't want to be an alarm clock that shouts any more.
CON: Because you keep reminding me and it gives me a headache.
ME: It gives me one too. So let's change: you choose when you sit down to study. What time suits you?
CON: Eight. Let me finish my episode first.
ME: If you start at eight you won't finish until ten and you'll be short of sleep. How about seven thirty? Save the rest of the episode for after homework; it's more fun then.
CON: Fine. Seven thirty.
ME: Set your own alarm. When it rings, you go by yourself and I won't say a word. And if it rings and you don't go? You make the rule.
CON: Then... no evening shows the next day.
ME: All right. It's your rule; I am only keeping it for you.
VS: Repeated reminders turn studying into Mum's job, with the child merely the one being pushed. The more pushed, the more the child drags to keep a little self-determination. Even primary schoolers need to feel they have a choice. Letting a child choose within limits, what time and what consequence, makes sitting down a promise to themselves. A promise to oneself weighs far more than another person's order.
BH: One reminder is an instruction. Ten reminders teach a child that the first nine do not count.
LN: Pick a relaxed moment, not a tense one, to set a study time your child chooses. Write it down and have them sign it. For the first week you do exactly one thing: do not remind.
CN: I don't want to be the shouting alarm clock any more. You choose the time.
TN: Right now, whose worry is my child's studying more: theirs or mine?
TN: Could I go three days without reminding? What makes that hard for me?

## Your child says the foundations are gone and studying is pointless
BC: Yen, in grade 8, gets a stomach ache whenever it is maths time. Her exercise book is blank. When Dad asks she says: I've lost the basics, I can't catch up now.
PX: You lost the basics because you're lazy. Sit down and work and you'll get it all back. Nothing is ever too late! | Dad doesn't understand that I already tried and it didn't go in. He thinks it's easy. There is no point telling him.
BA: You say you've lost the basics. Honestly, when do you think you stopped understanding?
CON: Grade 6. Around negative numbers and fractions.
BA: Grade 6 to now is two years. Two years sitting in class not understanding. I'd have a stomach ache too.
CON: When the teacher explains it sounds like a foreign language. And I can't even ask, because I don't know what to ask; I don't understand from the start.
BA: So asking you to do grade 8 work is like building the third floor when the first is missing bricks. You are not lazy; we are building in the wrong place.
CON: So what do we do, Dad?
BA: We go down to the first floor. I'll buy a grade 6 maths book and every evening we do exactly twenty minutes, starting from fractions. Nobody needs to know, so no embarrassment. In class you just do what you can; I'll speak with your teacher.
CON: Go back to grade 6? That's weird.
BA: I don't think so. What's weird is letting you sit four more years understanding nothing. Try it for two weeks; if it doesn't work we'll find another way.
VS: Maths and languages are built in layers: one missing layer makes everything above meaningless. A child who has lost the foundations is not short of willpower; they have nothing to hold on to. Every lesson confirms once more that they are bad at it, so avoidance becomes self-protection, sometimes showing up as real stomach aches and headaches. Going back to the exact gap, in small private doses, gives the child their first successes in a long time, and those successes are what bring motivation back.
BH: If the foundations are lost, go back to the foundations. Nobody builds a third floor on a first floor missing bricks.
LN: Find the gap together: turn back through earlier years' books until you reach a lesson your child can do smoothly. The starting point is right after it. Twenty minutes a day, no more.
CN: You are not lazy. We have been building in the wrong place. Let's go back to where you still understand.
TN: Do I know exactly which year and which topic my child's gap starts from?
TN: When my child says it won't go in, do I hear lazy or stuck?

## Your child leaves everything to the last minute
BC: A group presentation was set two weeks ago. At ten on the night before it is due, Hieu, in grade 10, finally opens his laptop, snapping and hammering the keys. Mum walks past, torn between pity and irritation.
PX: Two weeks of nothing and now you're up till dawn. Was I wrong? Serves you right! | When I am drowning, Mum stands on the bank saying serves you right. She wants me to lose so she can be right.
ME: (Puts a plate of fruit on the desk.) Tough night. I can't help with the work, but shall I make you something to drink?
CON: No thanks. I'm going crazy. I haven't touched my part at all.
ME: All right. I'll let you work. If it's not done by one, go to bed anyway; get up early and your head will be clearer.
CON: (Two days later, at dinner.) Our group got seven. My part was the sloppiest.
ME: Do you regret it?
CON: Yes. I knew I should start early, but every time I opened it I didn't know where to begin, so I closed it, and then I scrolled on my phone to forget.
ME: Ah, so it's not that you didn't want to do it. You opened it, it looked too big, and you felt swamped. Next time there's a big task, on the very first evening let's try just one thing: write five small steps on paper. Don't do any of them; just write them down.
CON: Only write them down? That's easy.
ME: Yes. It has to be easy to get started.
VS: Procrastination is rarely about time management. It is about avoiding an unpleasant feeling: the task is too big, too vague, or the fear of doing it badly. Each avoidance brings instant relief, which strengthens the habit. Scolding during the last-minute sprint only adds one more unpleasant feeling to avoid. Talking afterwards, and helping the child cut the big task down to a first step so small it is almost funny, reaches the root.
BH: While your child is sprinting, hand them water. Save the lessons for after the finish line.
LN: Next time your child has a big task, sit with them for five minutes on day one to write out the small steps and choose a first one that takes ten minutes. Do not do it for them; just ask: what is the smallest step?
CN: If it's too big, we cut it up. What is the smallest first step?
TN: Do I myself often leave things to the last minute? What do I need to hear then?
TN: Does my child put off everything, or only what feels hard and unclear?

## Your child says they do not want to go to school any more
BC: Monday morning, Nam, in grade 11, does not get up. Mum goes in; he pulls the blanket over his head: I'm not going to school any more. I'm sick of it. Mum is stunned. He has always been a decent student and never like this.
PX: Nonsense! If you don't study, how will you earn a living? Get up, you're late! | Mum only needs me to turn up at school. What is going on inside me, she does not want to know.
ME: (Sits on the edge of the bed, quiet for a moment.) You must be terribly tired to say that. It's not like you.
CON: I really am tired. Every morning I open my eyes and it feels heavy.
ME: Heavy how? I want to understand; I'm not making you get up right now.
CON: Extra classes until nine at night, homework until midnight. Up at five thirty. Sundays too. I don't know what I'm studying for any more.
ME: Hearing you, I realise your schedule is heavier than a working adult's. And we are the ones who built it. I am sorry I didn't notice you were this worn out.
CON: I don't really want to quit school. I just want to breathe.
ME: I understand. I'll ask your teacher for a day off today; get some proper sleep. Tonight the three of us will go through the timetable and drop whatever classes can be dropped. You choose.
VS: I'm not going to school any more, from a child who usually copes, is almost never laziness. It is an alarm: exhaustion, loss of meaning, or something at school the child has not mentioned. Long-term sleep loss in adolescence clearly reduces memory, concentration and mood. Forcing the child up is switching off the alarm while the fire keeps burning. Sitting down and asking heavy how is going to look for the fire.
BH: When your child says they do not want to go to school, the question is not how will you earn a living. It is what is making this so heavy for you.
LN: Write out your child's week hour by hour: school, extra classes, homework, sleep, play. Add up sleeping hours and free hours. If your child sleeps under seven hours a night, the first thing to cut is an extra class.
CN: You must be very tired to say that. Tell me about it.
TN: How many completely unscheduled hours does my child have each week?
TN: If my child were sick of school because of something there (friends, teachers), would they tell me?
