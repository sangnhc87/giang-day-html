CHUONG: When storms hit the family
EPI: Children can bear sad news. What they cannot bear is being left alone with things nobody explains.
LEAD: Parents fight, parents separate, money runs short, a relative falls gravely ill, a grandparent dies. At such times adults struggle with their own pain and think: the children are small, what do they know, don't worry them. But children hear everything through the wall and read everything on our faces, then fill the gaps with imagination, usually in the worst direction and often blaming themselves. This chapter is about telling children the truth at a level they can carry.

## You argued loudly and your child heard everything
BC: Ten at night, the parents argue in their room about money and in-laws. Voices rise; a door bangs. In the morning Mum sees Mi, in grade 4, with swollen eyes, eating breakfast in silence, glancing from Dad to Mum.
PX: That's grown-up business; children wouldn't understand. Eat up and go to school. Nothing is wrong! | Something is obviously wrong and Mum says nothing is. It must be terrible if it has to be hidden. They are probably splitting up, and probably because of me.
ME: (Sits beside her.) Mi, did you hear Dad and me talking loudly last night?
CON: (Nods, eyes brimming.)
ME: You must have been very scared. I'm sorry you had to hear it. Let me tell you what happened: Dad and I disagreed about money, we both got heated, and we raised our voices. The way we spoke to each other last night was not good.
CON: Are you going to split up? Na's parents argued and then they split up.
ME: No. We were angry about last night; we are not splitting up. People who love each other still argue sometimes, like you and Tho quarrel and then play again.
CON: Was it because of my piano lessons? I heard Dad say lesson fees.
ME: (Hugs her.) It was not because of you. Not one bit. Money is for Dad and me to work out. You did not cause it and you don't have to worry about it. You're still learning piano.
BA: (Comes over, sits on her other side.) I'm sorry too, Mi. I raised my voice last night. Mum and I have talked again, and this morning we're no longer angry with each other. See? (Takes Mum's hand.)
CON: (Lets out a long breath.) Then don't shout at each other tonight.
BA: We'll try hard. If we do get angry again, we'll go somewhere else to talk, and when it's over we'll tell you it's over.
VS: Small children assume they are the centre of everything, so when parents fight they very often believe they caused it. Nothing is wrong hides nothing; it only leaves the child alone with fear. The child does not need the details of the quarrel. They need three things: confirmation that what they heard was real, assurance that it was not their fault, and to see with their own eyes their parents making up. Conflict repaired in front of a child also teaches something precious: being angry does not end love.
BH: If your child heard you argue, your child also needs to see you make up. And needs to hear one clear sentence: it was not because of you.
LN: As a couple, agree on a pause signal for when a discussion heats up while the children are awake. And if a child has already overheard, the next day tell them together three things: it was real, it was not your fault, we have talked again.
CN: We were angry with each other; we are not leaving each other. And none of it was because of you.
TN: My child has seen us argue many times. Have they ever seen us make up?
TN: When we are at odds, do I draw my child onto my side by telling them the other parent's faults?

## You have decided to separate
BC: After two years of trying, the parents decide to divorce. Dad will move out next month. The two children, Nam in grade 7 and Na in grade 2, know nothing yet. The parents sit down together, this time not to argue but to discuss one thing: how to tell the children.
PX: It's because your father has someone else that I have to do this. (Or silence, leaving the children to find out when they see Dad carrying a suitcase.) | I must hate one of the two people who made me. Or: one morning Dad vanishes, nobody says anything, and the world can no longer be trusted.
BA: (The family in the living room on a Saturday afternoon, nowhere to go afterwards.) Mum and I have something important and sad to tell you both. We have decided not to live in the same house any more. We are getting divorced.
CON: (Na.) What is divorced?
ME: It means Dad and I won't be husband and wife any more and will live in two different homes. But Dad will always be your dad and I will always be your mum. That never changes.
CON: (Nam, voice hard.) Why? Whose fault is it?
BA: This is between Mum and me, between two adults. We tried for a very long time and can no longer live happily together. It is not one person's fault, and it is certainly not yours. You did nothing to cause this, and there is nothing you could do to change it.
CON: (Na, crying.) I'll be good. I won't fight with my brother over toys any more.
ME: (Hugs Na.) Na, whether you are good or not has nothing to do with it. You are the best thing Dad and I have together.
CON: (Nam.) So who do we live with?
BA: You both stay in this house with Mum and at the same school. I'm renting a place fifteen minutes away. On Wednesdays I take you to school, and every other weekend you're with me. Your room at my place, you choose the paint colour.
CON: (Nam, standing up.) I hate you both. (Goes to his room.)
ME: (To Dad, quietly.) Leave him. (A while later, knocks on Nam's door.) Nam, you are right to be angry. This is not fair to you. I'm not asking you to stop being angry. I'm just here.
VS: Research on children after divorce shows that what harms them most is usually not the separation itself but prolonged conflict between parents and being pulled into the middle: having to choose sides, carry messages, hear one parent run down the other. The best announcement is made by both parents together, with one shared explanation that blames nobody, plus concrete information about daily life: where the child will live, go to school, and when they will see each parent. Young children often believe they can fix it by being good. Older ones are often angry. Both reactions need to be received, not corrected.
BH: You may stop being husband and wife, but you never stop being parents. Do not make a child choose sides, and never speak ill of the other parent to them.
LN: If your family is in this situation, the two adults sit down and write together three sentences you will say and three things in the children's daily life that will not change. Agree on every word beforehand. Then each of you promises one thing: I will not use the children to pass messages.
CN: We will no longer be husband and wife, but we will always be your parents. And this is not because of you.
TN: Am I letting my child hear me criticise their other parent, even with just a sigh?
TN: After separating, what in my child's daily routine can I keep unchanged?

## Money is tight and your child wants what friends have
BC: Dad lost his job three months ago; the family lives on Mum's salary and shrinking savings. The parents have hidden it. Phat, in grade 8, has no idea, and tonight insists on a two-million-dong pair of boots because everyone on the football team has them.
PX: With what money! You think money grows on trees? The whole family is about to be on the street and you're still asking for things! | We're about to be on the street? What is happening? Asking for things makes me guilty. I am a burden.
BA: (Looks at Mum; she nods. Dad pulls out a chair.) Phat, sit down. There's something I should have told you sooner. I left my job at the company three months ago. They cut staff and I was one of them.
CON: (Stunned.) Three months? Then where have you been going every morning?
BA: Job hunting, and doing extra driving. I hid it because I didn't want you to worry, and honestly because I felt awkward in front of you. But you're fourteen; you have a right to know how things stand at home.
CON: So are we... okay, Dad?
BA: Things are tight, but we are not in danger. Mum still has her salary and I have two interviews lined up. We still have food on the table and you still go to school; those we guarantee. It's just that for a few months we don't buy what isn't really needed. The boots are in that group.
CON: I didn't know. I'm sorry, I thought...
BA: You've done nothing wrong. You didn't know, so you asked; that's normal. The fault is mine for not telling you. And the boots: I understand why you want them. Being the only one on the pitch without them is not nice.
CON: It's fine, I can still play in my old ones. I'll glue the sole. Dad, is there anything I can do to help?
BA: Just hearing you ask does me good. Earning money is our job; you don't carry that. But two things would help: turn off lights and fans when you leave a room, and on Saturday nights we'll cook at home instead of eating out, and you help Mum with one dish. As for the boots, when I'm working again we'll talk.
VS: Children sense financial strain at home even when nobody says a word. Hiding it completely leaves them confused, and when the truth bursts out in anger it arrives with fear and guilt. Better to tell the truth at a level that fits their age: what is happening, what is still guaranteed, what is changing for now. One important boundary: let the child know and contribute a small, manageable part, but do not make them carry adult worries or become the place where parents unload.
BH: Tell your child honestly that things are tight, say clearly what is still guaranteed, and give them one small way to help. Hiding it and then exploding in anger is the worst way.
LN: Whether money is plentiful or short, this week involve your child in one real spending decision: give them a fixed sum to shop for one family meal and let them work it out. Children learn the value of money through their hands, not through lectures.
CN: Things are tight but we are not in danger. Earning money is our job; you do not have to carry it.
TN: What does my child know about the family's real finances? Do they know it from what I tell them, or from the times I snap?
TN: Do I often use we work ourselves to the bone for you to make my child feel guilty?

## Grandpa has died and your child asks where he went
BC: Grandpa dies after two weeks in hospital. Bao, in grade 1, was his favourite; Grandpa collected him every afternoon. The family is busy with the funeral and the adults agree not to tell him, to say Grandpa has gone far away. Bao stands in the doorway and asks: where did Grandpa go, Mum? When is he coming to pick me up?
PX: Grandpa has gone on a long trip. Grandpa is having a long sleep. Children shouldn't ask so much; go and play outside. | Grandpa left without telling me; he must have stopped loving me. And sleep is frightening; you might not wake up.
ME: (Sits down and takes him in her arms.) Bao, I have something very sad to tell you. Grandpa has died. He was very ill; the doctors did everything they could, but his body could not get better. His heart stopped beating. He isn't breathing and he isn't in pain any more.
CON: What does died mean? Will he come back?
ME: Died means his life has ended. Someone who has died doesn't come alive again and can't come back. Grandpa can't pick you up any more. I know how much you love him.
CON: (Lip trembling.) But he promised to take me to see the fish on Friday.
ME: He promised, and he very much wanted to take you. He didn't leave you. He was ill, so he couldn't stay. He loved you so much; on his last day he was still asking whether Bao was home from school. (Mum cries.)
CON: Are you crying, Mum?
ME: Yes. I'm crying because I miss him too. When we're sad, we can cry; grown-ups cry too. If you want to cry, cry with me.
CON: (Cries for a while, then asks.) Was it because I was noisy the other day when he was sleeping?
ME: No. It was not because of you, or of anyone. Grandpa died of an illness. You made him happy every day; he told me so.
CON: Will you die, Mum?
ME: Everyone dies one day, but I am healthy and I plan to live a very long time to watch you grow up. Tomorrow we say goodbye to Grandpa. If you want to come, I'll hold your hand the whole time. Would you like to draw a picture for him to take along?
CON: I'll draw Grandpa and me going to see the fish.
VS: Children under seven or eight take words literally. Gone far away makes a child wait and feel abandoned. Sleeping can make a child afraid of bedtime. They need the real words, died, his body stopped working, he will not come back, said in a gentle voice. Young children grieve in short bursts: crying, then running off to play, then asking again weeks later. That is normal. Two questions nearly every child carries are was it my fault and will you die too, so answer them even before they are asked. Seeing adults cry and having a small part in the farewell teaches a child that sorrow is something we carry together.
BH: Tell your child the truth about death, in true words and a gentle voice. Let them be sad with the family; do not send them out to play so the adults can cry alone.
LN: If someone in the family has recently died, do one thing together to remember them: choose a photo for the table, plant something, tell a happy story about them at dinner. If not, when your child asks about death through a pet or a film, answer truthfully instead of changing the subject.
CN: Grandpa didn't leave you. He was ill, so he couldn't stay. If you're sad, cry with me.
TN: The first time someone in my family died when I was a child, what did the adults tell me? What did I understand?
TN: Do I allow myself to cry in front of my child?
