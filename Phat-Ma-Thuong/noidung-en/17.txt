CHUONG: Lying, forgery, taking things, excuses
EPI: A student who lies is afraid. Remove the fear first and the truth will come by itself.
LEAD: Lying to dodge blame, forging a parent's signature, taking a classmate's things, faking illness to get out: faults that touch honesty. Teachers get angriest here, feeling deceived. But heavy punishment only teaches the student to lie better. This chapter offers moves that create a safe way back so the student dares to tell the truth, while keeping the principle clear.

## A student lies to deny a fault
LOI: You clearly saw Viet tear a classmate's notebook, but he insists he did not. He looks straight into your eyes, perfectly composed. The class knows too.
CHIEU: Two chances to retell | Do not expose him. Say privately: I saw this, but I want to give you two chances to say it again. The first is right now. The second is until the end of today, when you come and tell me privately. If you retell it, I only need you to mend what you did and I will not punish you more for the first lie. If you do not, I will deal with it another way. The choice is yours.
THAY: Viet, I saw this. I give you two chances to say it again: now, or before the end of the day, come to me. If you retell it, I only need you to mend what you did, with no extra punishment for lying.
TRO: (Silent, then at day's end, comes.) Um... sir, it was me who tore it. I am sorry.
THAY: Thank you for coming back. You have told the truth; now let us think how to make it up to your classmate.
VS: He lies because he fears the consequence. A safe way back makes telling the truth cheaper than lying. A student who comes back himself remembers it longer than being exposed. Not punishing extra for the lie teaches that honesty has value.
RG: You must keep your word: if he retells it, no extra punishment. If he does not, deal with it under regulations without sarcasm. Do not set traps for him to lie more. When the fault is serious or someone is hurt, do not use this move to skip reporting.
BT: Keep a small slip reading: I want to retell it, which he can simply leave on your desk without speaking.
CN: I give you two chances to retell it, now or before the end of the day. Retell it and no extra punishment for lying.

## A student forges a parent's signature
LOI: You compare the signature in the contact book with earlier ones and it looks completely different. You ask gently; Khang stammers, then admits he signed it himself for fear his parents would see his poor mark.
CHIEU: A signature of his own | Do not shout. Say: you forged a signature, and that is serious, so I must say so plainly. But I want to understand why you were afraid. Give two tasks: one, tell your parents about this and the mark yourself, with you standing beside him if he wishes; two, he signs a line of his own below in the book: I promise to tell the truth about marks next time. His own signature replaces the forged one.
THAY: Khang, forging a signature is serious and I must say so clearly. But I want to understand why you were afraid. You tell your parents yourself, and I will stand beside you if you need.
TRO: Um... I was afraid they would shout, so I just signed.
THAY: I understand. Let us find a way for you to dare to tell next time. I will help you speak to your parents.
VS: Forging a signature almost always comes from fear of marks or of adults' reactions. Having him tell his parents with you helping lets him face the root fear. His own pledge signature in place of the forged one teaches more than any punishment.
RG: If he fears violence when his parents know, do not make him tell them at once; work with the school to ensure his safety. You must inform the family as regulations require, but prepare how to say it. Do not publicise the forgery.
BT: Set a time at the end of each month for students to report marks to their families with your support, rather than waiting for the contact book.
CN: Forging a signature is serious, but I want to understand why you were afraid. Let us tell your parents together.

## A student takes a classmate's things
LOI: Mai has lost fifteen thousand dong and a nice pen. You notice that Thanh, sitting nearby, has an identical pen. He goes red and says a friend gave it to him.
CHIEU: A door for returning, unseen | Do not interrogate or search bags. Tell the whole class gently: sometimes people pick up another's things by mistake. Open a returns box in a corner of the room; anyone who wishes to return something can put it in overnight, you will not ask names, and promise not to find out who. Privately to Thanh, say quietly: I do not believe you are a bad person. If there is something you need help with, come to me. In very many cases the item comes back within two days.
THAY: (To the whole class.) Sometimes people take another's things by mistake. I am putting a box in the corner; anyone who wants to return something can put it in. I will not ask names and I will not look for anyone.
TRO: (Thanh, privately.) Um... sir, may I return the pen to Mai?
THAY: You may. Thank you. If you want to tell me more, I am listening.
VS: Taking things often comes from need, envy or impulse. A way to return unseen lets him mend without being branded a thief. A student who returns of his own accord remembers the value of honesty longer than being caught.
RG: Do not search, do not name him before the class, do not accuse without certainty. If he took because of poverty, support him discreetly. If the sum is large or it repeats, tell the school and family, while preserving his dignity.
BT: Set up a lost-property box with someone in charge, so students have a place to hand in found items without being suspected.
CN: Sometimes people pick things up by mistake. There is a returns box in the corner, and I will not ask names.

## A student fakes illness or keeps asking to leave
LOI: Linh asks to go to the toilet almost every lesson, fifteen minutes each time. She often asks to go to the infirmary with a headache right at test time. You are suspicious but afraid she is truly in pain.
CHIEU: Exit cards | Give each student three exit cards a week; anyone needing the toilet, a drink or to wash their face places a card on the desk without asking. When the cards run out they ask your permission, which you consider privately. With Linh, ask once privately: you often feel unwell around tests; are you worried about something? If she is anxious about tests, help her find ways to stay calm before an exam.
THAY: Each of you has three exit cards a week. When you need to go anywhere, put a card on the desk, I will not ask. When they run out, ask me and I will consider it.
TRO: (Linh, privately.) Um... actually I am very worried about tests, sir, so I often get stomach aches.
THAY: Thank you for telling me honestly. Let us find a way to make you less anxious.
VS: Faking illness is often a way to escape anxiety. Exit cards give a legitimate right, so abuse of requests falls, and going out becomes controlled. Asking privately about test times touches the root.
RG: Do not refuse a student who is truly ill or has a health issue. Do not voice suspicion publicly. If anxiety is long-lasting, suggest the family and a counsellor. If a student has an underlying condition requiring frequent breaks, be flexible and note it.
BT: Replace the cards with small coloured wristbands, so you know at a glance without asking before the class.
CN: Each of you has three exit cards a week. Whenever you need to go, put one on the desk.

## A student makes excuses and blames others
LOI: Hoa does not hand in work and says a classmate lost it. Next day, the computer broke. The day after, her parents made her attend a memorial meal. Each reason sounds plausible, but that is three in a week.
CHIEU: Three words: I did | Do not rebut each reason. Hand her a sheet and say: I believe you have real difficulties. I ask you to write one of three phrases at the head of each reason: I forgot to do it, I did not get round to it, I chose not to. She chooses what is truest, and nobody grades it. You use the answer to make a plan together. Gradually she grows used to taking responsibility instead of pushing it outward.
THAY: Hoa, I believe you have real difficulties. Choose for me the most honest phrase: I forgot, I did not get round to it, or I chose not to. Nobody is grading this.
TRO: Um... I did not get round to it, because I was playing.
THAY: Thank you for telling the truth. Let us make a plan so you can hand it in tomorrow.
VS: Blame is a defence mechanism. Three options put her in the position of agent without being accused, and make the truth sayable. You use her answer to help rather than punish.
RG: Do not use it as a trap to make her admit fault. Respect her answer. If the real reason is family matters, switch to support. Do not make her answer public.
BT: Let her give a name to her favourite kind of excuse, like excuse number one, so she recognises the habit.
CN: Choose for me the most honest phrase: I forgot, I did not get round to it, or I chose not to.
