CHUONG: The days your child is not okay
EPI: Asking directly whether your child thinks about dying will not plant the idea. It opens a door for them to speak.
LEAD: This is the hardest chapter to write and the one most worth reading carefully. Some sadness a parent's love can hold. Some sadness has become illness and needs a professional, just as you cannot set your child's broken arm yourself. You do not need to become a psychologist. You need to do three things: recognise the signs, ask directly without panicking, and know which door to knock on. This chapter does not replace professional advice. If your child is in danger, take them to the nearest medical facility, or call 111 (Vietnam's national child protection hotline) or 115 (emergency).

## Your child has been sad for weeks and nothing interests them
BC: Trang, in grade 10, used to love drawing and laughed easily. For over a month she has stopped drawing, sleeps a lot yet is always tired, eats little, snaps at her brother, and her marks are falling. Asked, she says: I'm fine, just tired. Dad thinks she is lazy. Mum senses something is not right.
PX: At your age you have it so easy and don't know it. Eat and study, no worries; what is there to be sad or bored about? Get out and move and it will pass! | I have no right to be sad. My parents think I'm lazy. Telling them is useless, and I don't understand what is wrong with me either.
ME: (Brings two cups of warm milk, sits down beside her.) Trang, I'm not here about schoolwork. I've noticed that for more than a month you've changed: you've stopped drawing, you sleep a lot, you eat little. I'm worried, and I want to understand.
CON: I'm fine. I'm just tired.
ME: I believe you're tired. Is it the kind that goes after a good sleep, or the kind that stays however much you sleep?
CON: (After a pause.) The second kind. I open my eyes in the morning and I'm already tired.
ME: And drawing? You used to forget to eat when you were drawing.
CON: I don't enjoy it any more. I don't enjoy anything. It's like the colour has been switched off everything. I don't know why.
ME: The colour switched off. Thank you for telling me; I can picture it. You've carried that alone for over a month; that is heavy. I'm going to ask you something, and please answer truthfully; I won't panic: has there been a time when it felt so bad you thought you might as well disappear?
CON: (A long time.) ...A few times I thought it would be all right to sleep and not wake up. But I'm not planning to do anything.
ME: (Takes her hand, keeps her voice steady.) Thank you for being honest. I'm not angry and I'm not afraid of you. I don't think this is laziness or weakness. I think you are ill, like having a fever, but in the feeling part of you, and this illness can be treated. This week I'll take you to a doctor who specialises in this. I'll come with you, and I'll sit outside or inside, whichever you prefer.
CON: Am I crazy, Mum?
ME: No. Many people have this, adults and people your age. There is no shame in seeing a doctor. And until then, whenever that thought comes back, come and knock on my door, at any hour. Promise me.
VS: Sadness is part of adolescence. Depression is different: a sad or irritable mood nearly every day, loss of interest in things once loved, clear changes in sleep, appetite, energy and concentration, lasting two weeks or more and affecting daily life. In teenagers it often shows as irritability and tiredness, so it is easily mistaken for laziness or rudeness. It is a medical condition, not a character flaw, and it does not lift through willpower or advice to get out and move. Asking directly about thoughts of dying does not plant them. Studies suggest the opposite: people who are asked often feel relief that someone finally knows.
BH: Sadness beyond two weeks, loss of interest in what they loved, changed sleep and eating: this is no longer about temperament. It is time to see a doctor, as you would for a fever that will not come down.
LN: Compare your child today with three months ago in five areas: sleep, eating, interests, friends, marks. If three or more have clearly changed for over two weeks, book an appointment with the psychology or child psychiatry department of the nearest children's or psychiatric hospital.
CN: I think you are ill, not lazy. This can be treated, and I am coming with you.
TN: Am I calling a symptom of illness by the name lazy, rude or attention-seeking?
TN: Do I hold a prejudice about psychological or psychiatric care that makes me hesitate to take my child?

## Your child is so anxious that the body reacts
BC: Hai, in grade 8, is a strong student and a perfectionist. Lately he vomits before every test, and once in class his heart raced, he could not breathe, his hands went numb, and he thought he was dying and had to go to the school nurse. A heart check was normal. He now fears going to school in case it happens again.
PX: The doctor says there's nothing wrong with you. You're imagining it. Calm down; there's nothing to be afraid of. | It really happens and nobody believes me. I was dying and Dad says I imagined it. I must have something very serious the doctors can't find.
BA: Hai, the doctor says your heart is healthy, which is good news. But I know what happened in class was real. Racing heart, can't breathe, numb hands: you didn't make it up. I've read about it; it's called a panic attack.
CON: I thought I was dying, Dad. I couldn't breathe.
BA: It sounds terrifying. Here is how I've seen it explained: the body has a fire alarm. In real danger it rings so we can run: the heart speeds up, breathing quickens. In you, that alarm is too sensitive right now; it rings when there is no fire. When it rings it feels awful, but it does not kill you, and it settles by itself in about ten to fifteen minutes.
CON: But what if it happens again in class and everyone looks at me...
BA: You're afraid of it coming back, and that fear is more tiring than the attack itself. I understand. Let's rehearse something to do when the alarm rings. Breathe in through your nose counting to four, breathe out through your mouth very slowly counting to six. Try it with me.
CON: (A few breaths.) ...It's slowing a bit.
BA: Good. One more trick: look around and name five things you can see, four you can touch, three you can hear. It pulls your mind back into the room. And Hai, I think your alarm is this sensitive because you've been straining for too long. What do you think happens if you get an eight?
CON: Then I'm not good any more. And you and Mum are sad.
BA: Then this part is for us to make clear: if you get an eight, or a six, we are not sad with you. And I'll take you to see a psychologist. They have ways of training that alarm to be less sensitive, far more properly than I can. I'll also talk to your form teacher, so when you need to step out and breathe for a few minutes you can, with no questions.
VS: A panic attack is the body's alarm response switching on when there is no real danger. The symptoms are entirely real and very frightening: pounding heart, shortness of breath, numb hands, a sense of dying. The first step is still a medical check to rule out physical illness. After that, you're imagining it is the worst thing to say. Understanding the over-sensitive fire alarm helps the child fear the fear less. Breathing out longer than in, and naming what the senses notice, are simple, well-founded techniques. Anxiety that persists and leads to avoiding school is a reason to see a psychologist, and children respond rather well to therapy.
BH: The body's reaction is real; do not tell your child they are imagining it. Explain the over-sensitive fire alarm, teach them to breathe, and find someone qualified.
LN: Learn one breathing exercise with your child: in through the nose for four, out through the mouth for six, five times. Practise at ordinary times, each night before bed, so that in a tense moment the body already knows the way.
CN: What you felt is real. It is very unpleasant, but it will not kill you and it will pass. I'm here, breathing with you.
TN: Does my child have recurring physical symptoms before stressful events with no medical cause found?
TN: Which expectation at home keeps my child's fire alarm ringing?

## You discover your child is hurting themselves
BC: In sweltering heat Nhung, in grade 9, keeps wearing long sleeves. Mum happens to see when she reaches for something: several cuts on her forearm, some old, some new. Mum feels the ground give way.
PX: Oh my God, what are you doing! Are you mad? Do you want to kill us? What did we do for you to end up like this! | I am mad. I hurt my mother. Next time cut somewhere more hidden. Nobody understands that I do this to cope.
ME: (Steadies her own breathing first. Waits until evening, goes in, sits down, voice soft.) Nhung, this afternoon I saw your arm. I am not shouting and not panicking, I promise. I only feel love for you, because it must hurt terribly inside for you to do that.
CON: (Pulls her sleeve down, pale, silent.)
ME: You don't have to hide it from me any more. I'm not disgusted and not angry. May I look? I just want to be sure none of them is infected.
CON: (Hesitantly holds out her arm, bursts into tears.) I'm sorry, Mum...
ME: (Holds her.) You don't need to apologise. I'm the one who is sorry, that you hurt this much and I didn't know. I want to understand: when you do it, what does it do for you?
CON: When it's too suffocating inside and I can't bear it. One cut and it eases. It's like the pain all goes to one spot. I don't want to die, Mum.
ME: I understand. You found a way to get through the suffocating moments. I don't blame you. I only want to find you another way that harms you less. Let me ask one more thing to be sure, and please answer truthfully: have you ever thought about suicide?
CON: No... Sometimes I think it would be fine if I disappeared, but I'm not planning anything.
ME: Thank you for being honest with me. Then here is what we'll do. I'll take you to a doctor; people can help with this, and many young people your age have come through it. Meanwhile, when the suffocating feeling is about to become unbearable, come to me, or try gripping an ice cube tightly; it gives a strong sensation without leaving a mark. And let me look after the sharp things in your room for a while. It is not a punishment. It is me lending you a hand.
CON: Don't tell anyone. Dad, Grandma, my teacher...
ME: I will tell Dad, because he needs to know so we can care for you together, and he won't shout; I'll talk to him first. Apart from Dad and the doctor, I won't tell anyone without your agreement.
VS: Self-harm in teenagers is mostly not a suicide attempt, and not attention-seeking either. It is a way they have found to bring down emotional pain that is beyond bearing. But it signals serious distress, can worsen, and raises the risk of suicide later, so it always needs professional assessment. Panic, anger, or forcing a promise to stop at once usually only drives it into hiding. What helps: staying calm, asking what it does for the child, asking directly about suicidal thoughts, reducing sharp objects within reach, and getting the child to someone qualified.
BH: Seeing wounds on your child's arm, do not ask how could you do this to us. Ask what is hurting inside. Then take them to someone who can treat it.
LN: Read this dialogue once more and choose the first sentence you would say if one day you saw something similar. In shock, parents can only say what they have prepared. A suggestion: I've seen. I'm not shouting. It must hurt so much inside.
CN: I'm not shouting, not disgusted, not angry. It must hurt terribly inside for you to do that. Tell me.
TN: If I saw wounds on my child's body, would my first reaction be fear, anger or shame? What would I need to stay calm?
TN: At home, does my child have other ways to release the suffocating moments: a person to talk to, a quiet place, something to do?

## Your child says they do not want to live
BC: After a big argument over exam marks and a confiscated phone, Minh, in grade 11, texts Mum at midnight: I'm so tired. I'm sorry, Mum and Dad. The family would be better off without me. Mum reads it with ice-cold hands.
PX: Don't threaten us like that! Such a small thing and you talk about living and dying. Any more of that nonsense and you'll get a beating! | My parents think it's a threat. I'd have to really do it for them to believe me. There is nobody left to tell.
ME: (Wakes Dad; both go to his room at once. Knock, open the door. Minh is sitting on the bed, eyes red.) Minh, I read your message. We're here. We are not angry and we are not going to shout.
CON: (Head down, silent.)
BA: (Sits down beside him, speaks slowly.) I'm going to ask you one question and I need a truthful answer: are you thinking about suicide?
CON: (A very long time, then nods.) ...I've been thinking about it for weeks. Tonight the most.
BA: Thank you for telling me. Saying that takes real courage. I need to ask more, so I know how to keep you safe: have you thought about how? Have you prepared anything?
CON: I've thought... There are pills in my desk drawer that I collected from Grandma's.
ME: (Keeping her voice from shaking.) Let me look after those. I'll take them now. You are not in trouble for this.
CON: (Breaks down.) I'm sorry. I feel like a burden. I can't study, I keep disappointing you. Without me you'd suffer less.
BA: (Holds him, crying.) Minh, listen to me. You have never been a burden. Without you we would not suffer less; we would break in two. Marks, phones, everything I shouted about tonight, all of it together is not worth one of your fingernails. I said harsh things; I was wrong; I am sorry.
ME: Tonight we're staying here with you; nobody is going anywhere. You do not have to be alone. In the morning we'll take the day off and take you to hospital to see a specialist. What you are going through is a pain that has a name and people who can treat it. You don't have to hold it together alone any more.
CON: I'm so scared, Mum.
ME: I know. I'll lie here with you. You only need to get through tonight. Just tonight. We'll take care of the rest.
VS: Every statement about wanting to die or disappear must be taken as real. Assuming it is only a threat is the most dangerous mistake. What suicide prevention specialists advise: stay calm, ask directly using the word suicide, ask whether there is a plan and the means, listen without arguing or lecturing, remove medicines and dangerous objects, do not leave the child alone, and get them to specialist medical care as soon as possible. If the child has a specific plan or has already done something, go to emergency care immediately. A suicidal crisis is usually intense but limited in time. Holding your child through those hours and connecting them to help is how they are saved.
BH: When your child says they want to die, always believe it. Ask directly, remove what is dangerous, do not leave them alone, and get them to a doctor. Every other lesson can wait.
LN: Save in your phone now: 111 (Vietnam's national child protection hotline, free, day and night), 115 (emergency), and the number of the nearest hospital with a child psychiatry department. Check the family medicine cabinet: keep medicines locked or where your child cannot reach them alone.
CN: You have never been a burden. Tonight we are here with you. You only need to get through tonight.
TN: In anger, have I ever said something that meant we would be better off without you?
TN: If my child sent such a message, would I know at once what to do and whom to call?
