CHUONG: When parents are wrong: apologising and starting again
EPI: Children do not need perfect parents. They need parents who come back after hurting them.
LEAD: By now there have surely been moments that stung: you have said exactly the sentences in the red boxes, perhaps only yesterday. Do not use this book as a whip against yourself. No parent is never wrong. What separates a warm family from a cold one is not the number of clashes but whether, after each one, somebody comes back to reconnect. This last chapter is about reconnecting: apologising properly, changing an old habit, and starting again even when your child is nearly grown.

## You punished your child unjustly
BC: A five-hundred-thousand-dong note disappears from the table. Dad is sure Dat, in grade 6, took it, because only he was home. Dat denies it and cries; Dad becomes more convinced he is being stubborn, makes him kneel for an hour and bans football for a month. Three days later Mum does the laundry: the note is in Dad's own trouser pocket.
PX: (Quietly lets him play football again and buys him a milk tea, as if that settles it. Or: Well, you often lie, so of course I suspected you.) | Dad was wrong and says nothing. Adults who are wrong don't have to apologise. Or: I was wronged and it is still my fault.
BA: (Calls him over, sits at his level, puts the note on the table.) Dat, Mum found the note in my trouser pocket. You did not take it. I punished you unjustly.
CON: (Looks at the note, lips trembling.) I told you I didn't take it.
BA: You said it three times. You cried. And I didn't believe you. I made you kneel; I called you stubborn. I was completely wrong. I am sorry.
CON: (Sobs.) I knelt there and it was so unfair. I didn't do it and you didn't believe me.
BA: Wronged, punished, and believed by nobody: I can't imagine how that felt. Say all of it; I'm listening.
CON: You always think it's me. Whenever something goes missing you ask me first.
BA: ...Yes. You're right. I tend to think the worst of you first. That is something I must change, and not just this once. From now on, without evidence I don't accuse, and when you say no I believe you first.
CON: Do you mean it?
BA: I mean it, and you have the right to remind me if I forget. The football ban ends today. The hour you spent kneeling I cannot give back. I can only do one thing: this Sunday I'll come and watch you play, the whole match. And I'll tell Mum and Grandma that I punished you unjustly, so nobody still thinks you took it.
CON: (Wipes his eyes.) Tell Grandma too. She scolded me as well.
BA: Tonight, in front of you.
VS: Being punished unjustly is among the things children remember longest, because it strikes at the belief that the world is fair and parents are protectors. Making up for it with treats or leniency but no spoken apology leaves the lesson that the powerful need not admit fault. A real apology has four parts: say exactly what you did, acknowledge how the child felt, add no excuse, and say what you will do differently. One more part is often forgotten: restoring the child's good name with everyone who knew. Apologising does not cost a parent authority. Real authority comes from a child seeing that their parent is fair, even towards themselves.
BH: Apologise in four parts: what I did, how you felt, no excuses, and what I will do differently. Then restore your child's good name with everyone who knew.
LN: Think of a time you wronged your child and never apologised, however long ago. Tonight say: There is something I've thought over and I was wrong. I am sorry. No but, no explanation.
CN: I punished you unjustly. I was completely wrong. I am sorry.
TN: Did my own parents ever apologise to me? If so, how did it feel? If not, what did I wish for?
TN: When something happens, do I tend to think well or badly of my child first?

## You said something that cannot be taken back
BC: Furious because Huong, in grade 9, argued back, Mum blurts out: if I'd known, I'd never have given birth to you! Huong goes rigid and white and turns away without a word. For three days she has been strangely polite and obedient, and has not looked Mum in the eye.
PX: (Acts as if nothing was said. Or: I was angry and it just came out; it's nothing, why hold on to it?) | People tell the truth when they are angry. Mum wished I did not exist. I will be good and no trouble, so she regrets it less.
ME: (Goes into her room, sits down, voice catching.) Huong, I haven't slept for three days because of what I said. That I shouldn't have given birth to you.
CON: (Eyes on her book.) I'm fine. You don't need to say anything.
ME: You're not fine. I've seen you not look at me for three days. If my mother had said that to me, I think it would hurt until I was old. I'm not asking you to forget it. I'm asking you to hear the truth.
CON: (Silent, gripping her pen.)
ME: That was the cruellest sentence I have ever spoken, and it is not true. The truth is that on the day you were born I lay looking at you all night, afraid to sleep in case I blinked and you were gone. The truth is that I was angry that day and wanted to hurt you as much as I was hurting, so I chose the heaviest thing I could say. For an adult to do that is cowardly. I am sorry.
CON: (Tears falling on the page.) I thought you meant it. I thought I'd been so bad that you regretted me.
ME: (Holds her.) No. There has not been one day I regretted having you. Argue with me, misbehave, do anything at all, and I would still not trade you for anything. The only thing I regret is my own mouth.
CON: These few days I didn't dare do anything wrong. I was scared you'd hate me more.
ME: You do not have to be good to be loved by me. Argue and get things wrong as usual; I still love you. And I promise you one specific thing: when I am angry enough to say something terrible, I will leave the room. If I ever say something that hurts you, tell me straight away; don't carry it for three days like this.
VS: Things said in anger, I should never have had you, you're no child of mine, go wherever you like, are received by children as the truth slipping out, not as heat-of-the-moment words. A child who suddenly becomes obedient after such a sentence has not understood. They are afraid, and trying to earn the right to stay. Silence leaves the sentence lodged in the child for decades. Repeating exactly what you said, naming it as wrong, stating the opposite truth with a concrete detail, and committing to a different action next time: that is the only way to undo it.
BH: A cruel sentence spoken in anger does not fade by itself. It is undone only when the parent comes back, repeats it exactly, and says clearly: it was not true; I was wrong.
LN: If you once said something like that to your child, today is the day to go back, even if it was ten years ago. Your child still remembers. Say: There is something I once said that hurt you. It was not true. I am sorry.
CN: That sentence was not true. There has not been one day I regretted having you.
TN: Is there something my parents said in anger that I still carry today?
TN: Is my child well behaved because they feel secure, or because they are afraid?

## You realise you are repeating your own parents exactly
BC: Dad is scolding Tin, in grade 4, for spilling milk, grinding out each word. Mid-sentence he stops: the words he just used, the tone, the boy's shrinking eyes. Exactly Grandpa years ago, and himself at eight. He had sworn never to be like his father.
PX: (Brushes it aside: Grandpa was far harsher; this is mild. And carries on scolding.) | The child hears exactly what Dad heard as a boy: I am a nuisance, and adults are frightening.
BA: (Stops mid-sentence. Silent for a few seconds. Sits down.) Tin, I'm stopping. I am scolding you exactly the way Grandpa scolded me when I was your age.
CON: (Still scared, in a small voice.) Grandpa scolded you too?
BA: Very hard. Once I spilled a pot of soup and he scolded me all afternoon; I hid behind the chicken coop and cried. I was so afraid of him then, and I felt like rubbish.
CON: ...I was scared of you just now.
BA: (Chokes.) I know. I saw it in your eyes, because those were my eyes back then. I'm sorry, Tin. Spilled milk is tiny; we wipe it up and that's that. That tone just now was not because you were bad. It is an old voice inside me, and when I'm tired it switches itself on.
CON: So do you hate Grandpa?
BA: No. He loved me, but he knew only one way, the way his father taught him. I understood that when I grew up. And having understood, I want it to stop with me, not be handed on to you. Will you help me?
CON: How?
BA: Whenever I start grinding out my words, say: Dad, Grandpa's voice. When I hear that, I'll stop. I may not manage every time straight away, but I'll practise.
CON: (A shy smile.) Dad, Grandpa's voice.
BA: Yes, exactly like that. Now get a cloth and we'll wipe up the milk together.
VS: How we parent under stress usually comes not from what we have read but from what we received as children. Tired and angry, the brain runs down its oldest path, however firmly we swore to do otherwise. That is not fate. People who can see their own pattern and tell the story of their childhood coherently, both the pain and an understanding of their parents, are the ones able to break the cycle. Stopping in mid-scolding and naming it is the biggest step. And each time a parent comes back to repair after a slip, the child learns what the previous generation had no chance to teach: when you are wrong, come back.
BH: We do not choose how we were raised. But we choose what stops with us and what is passed on to our children.
LN: Write two columns. One: three things my parents did that I want to keep for my child. Two: three things I want to end with me. Read the page again whenever you feel your voice starting to grind.
CN: That tone just now was not because you were bad. It is an old habit of mine, and I am practising letting it go.
TN: When I am angry with my child, whose voice do I hear in mine?
TN: What did my parents do very well that I want my child to receive too?

## Your child is nearly grown: is there still time?
BC: Her son is seventeen and leaves home to study next year. Mum reads this far and realises that for more than a decade she has scolded more than listened and compared more than praised. The two of them now talk like housemates: have you eaten, are the fees paid. She wonders: it's too late, isn't it?
PX: (Says nothing, out of awkwardness, thinking he's grown and will understand how we feel. Or makes up for it with money and things.) | A child does not understand automatically. A child has only what was said. And he will carry this distance with him when he leaves.
ME: (One evening, brings fruit to his room. Stands awkwardly for a moment.) Khoa, I want to say something, and I'm not used to talking this way, so bear with me.
CON: (Takes off his earphones, slightly wary.) What is it? I haven't done anything.
ME: You haven't. It's me. You're leaving next year and I've been thinking back. All these years I've scolded you too much. I've compared you with your cousin Tuan endlessly. I've rarely sat and listened to you to the end.
CON: (Surprised, silent.)
ME: I did it out of worry; I was afraid you'd fall behind others. But that kind of worrying must have worn you out. I'm sorry. I didn't know another way; I'm only learning now.
CON: ...Why are you suddenly saying this?
ME: Because I'm afraid you'll leave before I've said it. There is something I have never said aloud: I am proud of you. Not for marks. Because you've taken the bus to school alone since grade 6 without one complaint. Because when Dad was ill, you quietly cooked for a month. I saw all of it, and all I ever talked about was what you hadn't done.
CON: (Turns his face away, voice rough.) I thought you didn't see those things.
ME: I saw. My failing was not saying so. I don't expect one evening to make up for seventeen years. I'm only asking for this last year: one coffee a week with you. Whatever you say, I'll listen, and I promise not to lecture.
CON: (A long pause.) ...Sunday mornings. But don't take me to your friends' cafes.
ME: Your choice of cafe. I pay.
VS: The relationship between parents and children does not close at any age. People still mend things with their parents at thirty and at fifty, and a late acknowledgement still has great healing power, because a child of any age is still waiting for it. What gives late words their value is being specific: saying exactly what you did, and exactly what good you saw in your child and never mentioned. Do not expect a warm response at once. A child used to distance needs time to believe the change is real, and the parent's steadiness afterwards is the real answer.
BH: No age is too late for a parent to say: I was wrong about this, and I have always seen this good in you. At any age, your child is still waiting for that sentence.
LN: Write your child a short handwritten letter with three things: one thing you regret doing, three specific things you see and value in them and have never said, and an invitation to do one thing together each week. Leave it on their desk. Do not ask whether they have read it.
CN: There is something I have never said aloud: I am proud of you, and I saw everything you did.
TN: What good in my child have I seen for a long time and never put into words?
TN: If my child left home tomorrow, what would I want the last sentence they heard from me to be?
