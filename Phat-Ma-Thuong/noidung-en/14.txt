CHUONG: A rotating toolbox: change moves weekly, never stale
EPI: Even the best move goes stale with constant use. A teacher needs a toolbox to rotate, and a way of choosing the right move for the right student.
LEAD: This chapter answers what many teachers confide: I punish often, but my ideas have run dry. It adds no new type of fault; it gives a method: how to change moves when the old one is stale, how to choose by class, by student temperament, by seriousness of the fault, and frames that can be varied endlessly. Each frame below is a machine that generates many moves.

## The old move has gone stale
LOI: In the first month of term, the Substitute-word jar is new and exciting. By the third month students are used to it and even cheeky about it. Teachers are bored and worried, as new ideas run low.
CHIEU: The wheel of moves | Make a paper wheel divided into eight sections, each a legitimate punishment you have used before. When a student errs, say: you choose three of the eight, I spin the wheel and pick one. He feels he takes part, and the spinning itself gives the punishment a game-like colour. Each month you replace two old sections with two new, so the wheel is always fresh.
THAY: This time you choose. Out of the eight sections of this wheel, pick three you could live with, then we spin and see where it lands.
TRO: Um... I pick numbers three, five, seven.
THAY: Good. I spin. It lands on five: you are the tree's guardian. I trust you to do it well.
VS: A move goes stale when it becomes familiar. The wheel brings in surprise and choice. A student who takes part in deciding the penalty resists less and remembers longer.
RG: Every section must be vetted by you in advance and respect the student. None may cause shame or exhaustion. Do not use the wheel for serious faults.
BT: Let the class suggest a new section for the wheel each month and you pick the suitable ones.
CN: You pick three sections you can live with, then we spin the wheel.

## New grade-ten class, nobody knows the routines yet
LOI: At the start of the year, a new grade ten has come from many schools. Many do not know the rules, are late, forget things, chat. Punishing from week one feels heavy; ignoring it risks loose discipline.
CHIEU: A grace week | Announce the first week is a grace week: every routine fault gets a gentle reminder and is noted in a small book called the lesson book, without punishment. At the end of the week read aloud the common faults without naming anyone, and the class together writes three rules for our class. From week two they apply the rules they themselves set, with the light punishing moves agreed.
THAY: This week is a grace week. We note faults in order to learn, with no punishment. At the end of the week the whole class will write three rules for our class.
TRO: Um... we write the rules ourselves, sir?
THAY: Yes. Rules you set yourselves, you keep yourselves.
VS: A new class needs time to learn the rules of play. A grace period lowers pressure and creates goodwill early in the year. Rules set by the class carry ownership and are respected more than rules imposed by the teacher.
RG: The grace week does not apply to dangerous behaviour or insulting a friend. Do not stretch the grace beyond two weeks. Keep notes to know which students need special attention.
BT: Make a class contract that every student signs and stick it in a corner of the room.
CN: This week is a grace week; we learn the rules and write the class rules together.

## A grade-twelve class under pressure
LOI: Grade twelve faces the final exam: tired, tense, easily irritated. They chat, doze, arrive late from extra classes. You want to keep order without adding pressure.
CHIEU: Punishment that costs no time | Use moves under one minute that help exam revision: a student who errs reads aloud a formula or a model paragraph for thirty seconds, or poses a multiple-choice question to the class. He loses no time, and the whole class gets extra review. Also set a convention: when the class is exhausted, you give a minute's rest to breathe, no reason needed.
THAY: You were noisy, so I ask you for a short job: read this formula aloud to the class for thirty seconds. The whole class reviews together.
TRO: Um... I will read it.
THAY: Thank you. Now the whole class breathes for one minute, then we carry on.
VS: Final-year students lack time most. Punishment must be a small unit of time and still useful for the exam. Giving a minute's rest when the class is exhausted shows them you understand.
RG: Do not add homework as punishment when they are already overloaded. Do not compare with other classes or threaten exam results. Watch for any student showing signs of burnout or heavy anxiety.
BT: Add a jar of encouragements; each student draws one at the end of the lesson.
CN: Please read this formula aloud for the class for thirty seconds.

## The fault has repeated for the fifth time
LOI: Tu has been late for the fifth time this month. You have reminded him, used the entry ticket and talked to him privately. He is still late. You are at a loss for another move.
CHIEU: The four-rung ladder | Use a ladder to know where you are: rung one, a gentle reminder with the eyes; rung two, ask the real reason; rung three, agree a small contract; rung four, invite the family to work on it together. Do not skip rungs and do not repeat a rung. When the fault recurs at a rung, go to the next, not increasing how severe the punishment is but increasing who solves it together. If Tu is at rung three or four, the change has to come from his circumstances, not from pressure.
THAY: Tu, we have tried many ways and it is still not working. I invite your parents to talk with you and me one session, not to scold, but to find a way together.
TRO: Um... I agree, sir.
THAY: Thank you. I believe your parents want you to be okay too.
VS: A fault repeating many times usually has a deeper cause. Increasing the severity of punishment does not address the cause. Adding people to untangle the cause is the right direction.
RG: When you invite the family, tell the student beforehand so he does not feel betrayed. Do not use the family as a threat. If there are signs of violence at home, think carefully before inviting them.
BT: Record the rungs already climbed in the notebook, so next time you do not start from the beginning.
CN: We have tried many ways; now I invite your parents to think of a way together with you.

## A student who is naughty but very bright
LOI: Quang is the brightest in the class and also the naughtiest. He finishes work early and then teases friends and argues with you. Ordinary punishments he treats as games.
CHIEU: The student designs the punishment | Challenge him: I believe you are smarter than the moves I have. Design a punishment for the fault you just committed, by three criteria: fun, useful, and making you not want to repeat it. Show me; I approve or adjust. He sees how heavy or light it is, and often sets a harder punishment than you had planned. Caught up in designing, he shifts his energy to creating.
THAY: Quang, I challenge you: design a punishment for the fault you just committed, with three criteria: fun, useful, and not wanting to repeat it. Show me at break.
TRO: Um... let me think. I might come up with a punishment heavier than yours, sir.
THAY: All the better. I approve it, and if it is good I will use it for the whole class, with your name as author.
VS: A bright student is bored by ordinary moves because they are too easy. Giving him the right to design turns the fault into a problem and you into a collaborator. He makes the rules for himself, so it is hard to resist.
RG: You keep the final say. Do not let him choose a punishment that harms himself or others. Do not use this with a student carried away by an excessive sense of power.
BT: Collect the good punishments designed by students into a class book of moves.
CN: Design a punishment for this fault yourself, with three criteria: fun, useful, and not wanting to repeat.

## Frames you can vary endlessly
LOI: A teacher wants a new idea every day but cannot keep producing them. Looking at the moves in the book, he sees they share patterns. He wants to know how to generate new ones himself.
CHIEU: Twelve frames | Every move in this book belongs to one of twelve frames. One, free talk with a time limit. Two, swap roles to feel a friend's feeling. Three, give a post matching the student's need. Four, repay a debt of time with a kind act. Five, guardian of an object or task for a month. Six, a card or short letter to the person affected. Seven, teach a lesson or skill back to a friend. Eight, design your own punishment. Nine, draft and upgrade. Ten, a small signed contract. Eleven, a jar or log in place of reminders. Twelve, an exemption slip and the right to choose. Facing a new fault, pick a frame and fit it to that fault.
THAY: (Asking himself.) What kind of fault is this? Lack of attention, the post frame. Hurting a friend, the card and role-swap frames. Repeated, the log and contract frames. Pick a frame and fit it to the fault.
TRO: (A student, later.) Teacher's punishments are always different but always make sense.
THAY: Yes, because I always ask myself: what is this student lacking?
VS: Every good move comes from one root idea about the student's need. Once a teacher holds the twelve frames, thinking up new moves is like fitting pieces together. The moves do not run dry because students' needs are endless.
RG: Every move, however new, must pass three tests: private, tied to the fault, and ending in something useful or a smile. A move that fails is dropped.
BT: Each term, swap ten new moves with colleagues and record them in the department's book.
CN: What is this student lacking in this fault? I pick the frame that fits and we do it together.
