CHUONG: When the school calls
EPI: Between your child and the teacher you need not pick a side. Stand on the side of the truth, and stand next to your child.
LEAD: The form teacher's number lights up and a parent's heart is already racing. Your child has been punished, has cheated, has been rude to a teacher, or comes home saying the teacher was unfair. Parents tend to lean one of two ways: defend the child fiercely, or join the school and scold the child a second time. Both spoil things. This chapter looks for a third way: hear both sides, protect your child's dignity, and let them step up to put it right.

## The teacher calls to say your child cheated
BC: At six in the evening the form teacher calls: during a history test Ha, in grade 8, was caught with notes open under her desk. A report was filed and she was given zero. Ha comes home as if nothing happened.
PX: You have shamed us! This family may be poor but nobody has ever been dishonest. Where did you learn that? | I am the family's shame. I am a dishonest child. What hurts my parents is their own honour.
ME: (Waits until she has eaten.) Ha, your teacher called me this afternoon. I want to hear it from you first, start to finish, in your own words.
CON: (Turns pale.) ...I opened my notebook. The teacher caught me.
ME: Thank you for telling the truth straight away, without denying. How did you feel when she caught you?
CON: I wanted to sink into the floor. The whole class turned to look.
ME: It can't have been easy since then either. I'm asking this not to find you an excuse: why did you open the notebook that day?
CON: I hadn't learned the lesson. The night before I did maths until late and had no time for history. I was scared you'd scold me for a bad mark.
ME: So you chose to cheat because you feared a bad mark more than you feared cheating. I need to look at how I react to marks. As for your part, let me be clear: the zero is deserved and I will not ask the teacher to change it. One honest bad mark is worth more than ten borrowed nines.
CON: I know, Mum.
ME: What do you think you should do with your teacher tomorrow?
CON: Apologise. And ask if I can retake the test, whatever mark I get.
ME: You thought of it yourself, so you do it yourself. I trust you can.
VS: Most students who cheat do not despise honesty. They cheat when the pressure to have a mark is greater than their belief that they can manage alone. Consequences from the school are necessary and parents should not plead for leniency. But another round of humiliation at home only teaches a child to cheat more discreetly. Why did you open the notebook that day leads to the root, and letting the child propose the repair lets them win back self-respect through action.
BH: Do not ask for the consequence to be reduced, and do not add humiliation. Consequences teach responsibility. Humiliation only teaches concealment.
LN: Say this at an ordinary time, before anything has happened: We would rather receive an honest three than a fake nine. Then prove it by how you react to the next bad mark.
CN: One honest bad mark is worth more than ten borrowed nines.
TN: Is the pressure for marks at home strong enough that cheating looks like a way out?
TN: Do I cheat in small ways in front of my child: lying about their age to avoid a ticket, running a red light when the road is empty?

## Your child comes home saying the teacher was unfair
BC: Phong, in grade 7, comes home red in the face: the teacher made me stand at the back for the whole lesson and I didn't do anything! Duc behind me poked me first, I turned around, and she only saw me.
PX: Version one: If the teacher punished you she was right; teachers are never wrong; you must have done something. Version two: What kind of teacher is that? Tomorrow I'm going in to sort this out! | Version one: my parents never believe me. Version two: whenever something happens Mum goes to school, so I never need to handle anything myself.
BA: Standing a whole lesson when you feel wronged is infuriating. Tell me from the beginning, slowly.
CON: Duc poked my back with a pen three times. The third time I turned and told him to stop. Right then she looked. She said I was disrupting the class and made me stand, and wouldn't let me explain.
BA: Not being allowed to explain is the worst part. I believe your account. I'm also wondering: from where she stood, what could she see?
CON: ...She only saw me turned around talking.
BA: Yes. So she punished what she saw. She was missing half the story; that doesn't mean she dislikes you. How would you like this to end?
CON: I want her to know I wasn't being disruptive for no reason.
BA: That's fair. When and how do you think she would listen?
CON: Probably after class, when she isn't busy. I'd say: Miss, yesterday I turned around because my classmate was poking me. Next time I'll raise my hand and tell you instead.
BA: That's a good sentence; it covers what was unfair and owns what you could have done better. You speak to her first. If you've done that and it's still not right, then I'll meet her.
VS: When a child reports being wronged at school, the parent holds half the story, and so does the teacher. Declaring the teacher right leaves the child without support. Declaring the teacher wrong costs the child respect for the person teaching them and the chance to practise speaking up. Believing the child's feelings, then helping them see from the teacher's position, then letting them go and speak, is valuable practice in defending oneself politely. Step in directly only when the child has tried and failed, or when the matter is serious, such as insults or violence.
BH: Believe your child's feelings, but do not hurry to give the verdict. Teaching a child to speak up politely serves them better than speaking for them.
LN: Next time your child complains about a teacher, ask three questions in order: What happened? From where the teacher stood, what did she see? How would you like this to end? Then rehearse together what they will say.
CN: I believe you. Now let's look from where the teacher stood and see what she could see.
TN: Am I the type who always sides with teachers, or always with my child? Where does that come from?
TN: Can my child explain themselves when misunderstood, or do they always wait for us to step in?

## The school summons you because your child hit a classmate
BC: The discipline master calls: Kiet, in grade 9, punched a boy at break and made his nose bleed. Parents are to come in tomorrow. Dad rides home with shaking hands. Kiet is already sitting in the living room, face set hard.
PX: Did I send you to school to hit people? Tomorrow you will kneel and apologise! Thug! | Dad doesn't care why. I am a thug. Nobody in the world is on my side.
BA: (Sits down opposite, breathes out.) I've heard from the school. I am angry; I won't hide it. But I haven't heard you yet. What happened?
CON: I punched him. That's all. Go on, shout.
BA: I'm not going to shout. I've known you for fourteen years; you are not a boy who punches people for nothing. Something must have happened.
CON: (A long silence, voice thick.) He brought up Mum leaving. In front of everyone. He said my mum ran off with a man.
BA: ...I understand now. Hearing that would make my blood boil too. It hurts you there, I know, because it hurts me there too.
CON: I couldn't take any more, Dad. He's been saying it all week.
BA: A whole week and you bore it alone. I wish you had told me sooner. Listen to two things, both true. One: he was wrong, he insulted our family, and tomorrow I will say so clearly to the school; that has to be dealt with. Two: the punch was still yours, and he really bled. That part you must own.
CON: I own it. I'm sorry for hitting him. But I'm not sorry for being angry.
BA: Right. Being angry is not wrong; hitting is. Tomorrow say it exactly like that. I'll be sitting next to you.
VS: Violence from a child who is not usually aggressive almost always has a trigger, often being struck where it hurts most. Asking what happened before judging is not indulgence; it is looking for the whole truth. The child needs to hear two things together: your feeling is legitimate, and your action has consequences you must bear. Without the first the child feels abandoned. Without the second the child learns that pain is a licence to hit.
BH: Every feeling is allowed. Not every action is. Your child needs to hear both halves from you.
LN: Teach your child an exit line for when they are provoked to the limit: I'm not discussing this. Then turn and find an adult. Practise it like a martial arts drill, aloud, several times.
CN: Being angry is not wrong. Hitting is. That part we will go and repair together.
TN: When I hear my child has caused trouble, what is my first thought: are they all right, why did they do it, or what will people think of me?
TN: Is my child silently putting up with something at school that I do not know about?

## The teacher says your child is rude; your child says the teacher hates her
BC: The literature teacher messages Mum: Trang, in grade 10, answers curtly, gives sideways looks, and walked out when corrected. Asked about it, Trang says: that woman has hated me since the start of the year; whatever I do she picks at.
PX: Is that how you speak about your teacher? Who taught you to call her that woman? Being rude to her is being rude to me! | Mum only heard that woman; she didn't hear what happened to me. One more adult who won't listen.
ME: I hear you say she has disliked you since the start of the year. Something must have made you feel that. Tell me the first thing you remember.
CON: In the second week she read my essay to the class as an example of going off topic. Everyone laughed. Since then I haven't wanted to say anything in her lessons.
ME: Having your essay read out like that is really humiliating. I see why you closed up. And yesterday?
CON: She asked why I wasn't taking notes. I said I had. She said what attitude is that. I got so angry I walked out.
ME: One more question, not to catch you out: when you answered her, what were your voice and your eyes like?
CON: ...Probably not very nice. I gave her a look.
ME: Yes. So there are two layers. The old one: she embarrassed you and it still hurts. The new one: you answer with attitude and she feels disrespected. The two of you keep pushing each other away. One of you has to stop first.
CON: Why can't it be her?
ME: Because she doesn't know how much that essay hurt you. If you don't tell her, she never will. I won't force you. But think: do you want every literature lesson for the next two years to be a battle, or would you try telling her once?
CON: ...Let me think. But walking out of class was wrong, I know.
ME: For that, apologise to her first; it needn't wait until you've thought through the rest. And at home I ask one thing: call her your teacher.
VS: Long conflicts between a student and a teacher often begin with a small hurt that was never spoken, after which each reads everything the other does in the worst light. If parents only correct the way the child refers to the teacher, the conversation dies at the first line. Hear the original story first, then help the child see their own contribution, and only then restate the boundary about courtesy; that way the child can take in all three.
BH: Listen first, limits after. A child who has been fully heard finds it much easier to admit their own part.
LN: If your child has a grievance with a teacher, help them write three lines: what upset me, what I hope for, what I will do differently. They may give it to the teacher or keep it. Writing it down is already half the untangling.
CN: I'll hear your side first. Then we'll talk about how you answered your teacher.
TN: When my child speaks badly of a teacher, do I respond to the attitude first or the hurt first?
TN: When did I last speak with my child's teacher when there was no incident?
