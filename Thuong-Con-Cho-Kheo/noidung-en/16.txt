CHUONG: Low self-worth, looks, and the times your child falls
EPI: Children learn to talk to themselves in the voice their parents used to talk to them.
LEAD: Some children look fine outside while inside a voice keeps criticising: I'm ugly, I'm stupid, I'm worse than everyone. That voice does not appear from nowhere. It is pieced together from classmates, social media, and things parents let slip without imagining a child would keep them. This chapter is about how to respond when your child runs themselves down, loses, or is left out, so that the voice in their head gradually becomes a kinder one.

## Your child says I'm ugly
BC: Nhi, in grade 8, stands in front of the mirror for a long time and bursts into tears. She has acne and a rounder body. Today a classmate called her a pig. She tells Mum: I'm so ugly. I hate my face. I don't want to go to school.
PX: You're not ugly at all; my daughter is the prettiest. Though you should cut down on snacks and exercise, and you'll slim down fast. | Mum says prettiest just for the sake of it, because straight afterwards she tells me to lose weight. So Mum thinks I'm fat too.
ME: (Sits on the bed beside her, in no hurry to soothe.) Someone called you a very ugly word. Hearing that and still having to sit through the rest of the day is heavy.
CON: She said it in front of everyone. They laughed. And she's right; look at me, my face is covered in spots and I'm fat.
ME: I am looking at you. I see a fourteen-year-old who is growing; your skin is changing because of puberty, your body is changing because of puberty. At your age my forehead was covered in spots too; I wore a fringe to hide it. Grandma has photos; I'll show you.
CON: (Quieter.) You had them too?
ME: Badly. They only eased in grade 11. Let me tell you two true things. One: acne can be treated; this weekend I'll take you to a dermatologist, and please don't buy creams online. Two: whatever your skin is like, that word she used is wrong. It says something about her rudeness, and nothing about you.
CON: But I really am fat.
ME: Your body is growing, and it is healthy: it can run, swim, and hug me. I don't want you hungry or skipping meals to please a rude person. If you want to feel fitter, the two of us can walk every afternoon; I need it too. But that is for health, not as an answer to her.
CON: ...I still have to see her tomorrow.
ME: Yes. If she says it again, look straight at her, say that's rude, and walk away. And if she keeps repeating it, I'll speak to your teacher. Mocking someone's looks is bullying, not joking.
VS: Puberty is when children are most sensitive about appearance, and their brains magnify the feeling that everyone is looking at them. A hurried you're pretty does not land, because it denies what the child sees in the mirror. Advice to lose weight at that moment confirms the fear and can be the starting point of dangerous dieting. What helps is acknowledging the hurt, normalising the changes of puberty, talking about the body in terms of what it can do, not how it looks, and dealing with what can be dealt with, such as acne.
BH: Do not argue with how your child feels about their looks, and never criticise their body. Talk about what their body can do, and call an insult by its name: the speaker's rudeness.
LN: Review how people talk at home for a week: does anyone call themselves fat, call others ugly, use nicknames about appearance? That includes you criticising yourself at the mirror. Your child is learning how to see bodies from all of it.
CN: That word says something about the rudeness of the person who said it, and nothing about you.
TN: Do I often comment on weight and looks, my child's, my own, people on television, in front of my child?
TN: Is my child skipping meals, eating very little, or preoccupied with weight?

## Your child loses and wants to quit
BC: Tung, in grade 5, has trained at chess for two years. At his first district tournament he loses three games out of four. He is silent in the car. At home he throws the chess set into a corner: I'm not playing any more. I'm rubbish.
PX: One loss and you quit. What kind of boy is that weak? I've paid two years of fees; you can't stop halfway! | Losing is shameful, and being sad about losing is shameful too. Dad cares about the money, not about me.
BA: (Says nothing in the car. At home, lets him lie down for a while before going in.) Heavy day. Three losses, and in every one you sat there until the last move.
CON: I'm so bad, Dad. A kid a year younger than me beat me.
BA: I watched that game. At move twenty you were still a piece ahead. I don't know chess well, but I didn't see a bad player. I saw you trembling.
CON: ...Because I was scared of losing. Then I played the wrong move.
BA: First tournament; who wouldn't tremble? In my first village football tournament I sent a penalty clean off the pitch and the whole village laughed. I went home and stayed under the blanket for two days.
CON: (One eye opens.) Did you ever play again?
BA: Two days later I missed it too much and went back. Listen: you don't have to decide anything tonight. A person who has just lost decides badly. Be as sad as you need. Next week we'll see: if you want to stop, stop; I won't push. I only ask one thing before you decide: go over game three with your coach and see at which move you lost it.
CON: What for?
BA: So that if you stop, it's because you no longer like chess, not because of one unlucky day. Those are very different things.
CON: (A week later.) Dad, I went over it. I lost because I traded queens too early. Coach says that can be fixed. I'll keep training until the next tournament and then see.
VS: A first failure in public is a very big experience for a child. At that moment they need no lecture on perseverance, and certainly no reminder of the fees. A child who says I quit is usually saying it hurts too much and I am afraid of being hurt again. Three things help them up: acknowledging the sadness without belittling it, an adult telling a failure of their own, and separating the decision from the emotion by postponing it. Reviewing the defeat as a specific lesson turns failure from a verdict into information.
BH: Do not let your child decide to quit on the day they lose. That day, just sit beside them. Leave the decision for next week, when the pain has become a lesson.
LN: At dinner tonight, tell your child about a time you failed badly and how long it took you to get up. Include the part where you wanted to quit. Your child needs to know that the adults they look up to have fallen the same way.
CN: You don't have to decide anything tonight. Be as sad as you need. Next week we'll see.
TN: When my child loses, am I sad for them or for my expectations?
TN: Is our home a safe place to fail, or only a place to bring achievements back to?

## Your child says they can't do anything right
BC: Uyen, in grade 7, drops a bowl of soup, wipes it up and sinks to the floor: I ruin everything. I'm useless. Mum has noticed that lately she often calls herself stupid and good for nothing, and scolds herself harshly for any slip.
PX: What nonsense! You're capable; who says you're useless? Don't think so negatively. | Mum brushes it off. She has no idea how loud it is in my head. And now I've got one more thing wrong: thinking negatively.
ME: (Sits down on the floor beside her.) You dropped a bowl of soup and called yourself useless. I've heard that a few times now. Is there often a voice like that in your head?
CON: (Surprised to be asked.) ...Yes. Whenever I get something wrong it says it.
ME: What does it say?
CON: You're so stupid. You can't even do that. Everyone else can; only you can't.
ME: Goodness, it's harsh. Let me ask you: if your best friend Thu dropped a bowl of soup, would you say those things to her?
CON: No. I'd say never mind, just wipe it up.
ME: Then why speak more harshly to yourself than to a friend? You deserve to hear never mind, just wipe it up as well.
CON: (Silent.) I don't know. It just comes out.
ME: I think I know where it learned that. When you were little I often said you can't even do that. I said it when I was tired; I never thought you'd keep it. I'm sorry. That voice is mine when I'm cross; it is not the truth about you.
CON: (Eyes filling.) You remember saying it?
ME: Hearing you say it back, I remember, and it hurts. Let's practise this: every time that voice starts, add a sentence from Thu. You're so stupid, and then straight after: never mind, we can fix it. It feels strange at first; with practice it becomes normal. I'll practise with you; I have that voice in my head too.
VS: The way a child speaks to themselves is largely recorded from how important adults spoke to them, especially when they made mistakes. Telling a child not to think negatively does not switch the voice off; it only gives them one more reason for self-blame. What works is helping the child notice the self-critical voice as something separate from themselves, then practising answering it in the voice they use with a close friend. A parent reclaiming their own words is a great gift: the child understands the voice is not the truth; it is just an old sentence.
BH: Your child speaks to themselves in the voice adults once used with them. To change that voice, change yours first, and teach them to talk to themselves as they would to a best friend.
LN: Next time your child makes a small mistake, spilling, forgetting, getting something wrong, say exactly one sentence: Never mind, we can fix it. Repeated often enough, it becomes the voice in their head.
CN: Speak to yourself the way you speak to your friend: never mind, we can fix it.
TN: When I make a mistake, what do I say to myself? Can my child hear it?
TN: What do I say when I'm cross that I would not want to become the voice in my child's head?

## Your child will not try anything new
BC: The school opens a drama club; the teacher says Khai, in grade 6, has a good voice and invites him to join. He loves the idea and secretly rehearses lines in his room, but absolutely refuses to sign up: what if I'm bad and they laugh at me? Mum knows he is like this with everything: swimming, football, art contests.
PX: There's nothing to be scared of; be bold! A boy this timid will get nowhere. I've signed you up; you go tomorrow! | Mum thinks I'm timid. She pushes me into the thing I fear most without asking. Tomorrow I'll pretend to be ill.
ME: I heard you rehearsing in your room; I listened at the door for a moment, and it was very good. You really like drama and you're afraid of being laughed at. Are those two having a tug of war inside you?
CON: Yes. I do like it. But in front of people I go completely stiff.
ME: Stiff how? Tell me.
CON: My heart pounds, my hands get wet, my head goes blank. In grade 3 I recited a poem in front of the school and forgot it, and the whole school laughed. I still remember it.
ME: Something from grade 3 is still following you. No wonder. I won't make you sign up. Let me ask: if going on stage is step ten, what is step one, the step that feels a bit scary but that you can do?
CON: ...Acting it for you.
ME: Good, step one. Step two?
CON: Acting it for you and Dad and my little sister. Step three is probably going to watch them rehearse, just standing outside.
ME: Nice. We'll go one step at a time; if a step isn't ready yet, you stay on it and nobody hurries you. Step one tonight; I'll be the audience, and I promise not to laugh unless the play is a comedy.
CON: (Laughs.) It is a comedy, Mum.
ME: Then I'll laugh as hard as I can.
VS: A child who avoids challenges is usually not short of ability but protecting themselves from an old humiliation. Each avoidance strengthens the fear, because the child never gets to find out that they can cope. Pushing them straight into what they fear most can easily create another bad memory. Leaving them be lets the fear grow. The middle way is breaking it into steps the child sets themselves, each a little hard but doable. Each step passed is new evidence replacing the old memory, and real confidence is built from that evidence, not from encouragement.
BH: Do not push your child into the water, and do not let them stand on the bank forever. Lay out the steps together, and let them do the stepping.
LN: Pick one thing your child wants to do but does not yet dare. Draw a five-rung ladder together: the top rung is that thing, the bottom rung something they can do this week. Stick it on the wall. They tick each rung themselves.
CN: We'll go one step at a time. If a step isn't ready yet, you stay on it and nobody hurries you.
TN: Am I the pushing type or the shielding type? Is that helping my child?
TN: Is there a childhood humiliation I still carry today?
