CHUONG: Chatting and disruption
EPI: A good punishment is one the student retells to friends with a smile, and still remembers not to repeat.
LEAD: Every lesson brings small things: two students whispering, one turning round, the whole class erupting in laughter. Heavy punishment feels excessive, but ignoring it lets the class drift away. This chapter gathers short, funny, private punishments that embarrass no one and, above all, turn the fault into something useful. Each comes with the reason it works and the limit that tells you where to stop.

## Two students whisper all lesson
LOI: Two students at the back chat for most of the lesson. You have reminded them twice; each says yes, and five minutes later they are whispering again. They are not loud, but they steadily eat into the concentration of those around them.
CHIEU: Ten minutes unleashed | At the end of the lesson, invite the two to stay for the first three minutes of break. Say: you two have so much to talk about, so I am giving you exactly three minutes to say it all, as freely as you like, and I will time it. When three minutes are up, that is enough. They are being heard, and they notice they are repaying a debt of time taken from the whole class. You may add: next time something cannot wait, write it on paper and at break I will listen to your representative.
THAY: Would you two stay with me for three minutes? I am not angry. I just want to hear whatever you have not finished saying. Ready? Three minutes.
TRO: Um... you are letting us talk, sir?
THAY: Go ahead. Three minutes, as much as you like. When time is up I will ask for your full attention next lesson. Deal?
VS: Students chat because they have something real to say and cannot wait. This move acknowledges that need and then attaches a concrete time limit. The two are not shamed before the class, and when given free rein most feel awkward and stop before the three minutes are up.
RG: Do not punish in front of the class. Do not ask what they were saying. If the talk is about something serious, such as a friendship quarrel or trouble at home, stop treating this as punishment and turn it into a real conversation.
BT: If they are shy about staying, change it to: stack the chairs and tables at break, talking as they work. Or let them choose: three minutes of free talk, or three minutes of true silence next lesson.
CN: I know you two have plenty to say. I will give you time to say it all, and then when lesson time comes we learn.

## The whole class bursts out laughing and cannot stop
LOI: A student accidentally says something funny mid-lesson and the class roars. You wait, but the laughter drags on; a few even repeat the line for another round. A lesson that was going well has been cut in two.
CHIEU: Thirty seconds of controlled laughter | Smile and say: that really was funny, so I give the whole class exactly thirty seconds to laugh as loud as you can, and I will time it. When time is up, raise a hand: stop. The class may laugh a little more, but it fades. Say: thank you, now we carry on. If someone is still laughing, just remind them lightly: you still owe me ten seconds.
THAY: That line deserved a laugh. Thirty seconds, go! ... Time. Thank you, everyone, now back to the lesson.
VS: Laughter cannot be crushed by an order to be quiet; the more you forbid it, the more it explodes. Allowing it within a clear window makes it something you control. The class also sees you as someone with a sense of humour, and cooperates more next time.
RG: Do not allow laughter aimed at one particular student in a mocking way. Stop it at once and speak privately to the one laughed at.
BT: Use a small ritual to reset after laughing: the whole class takes three deep breaths before you read the next question.
CN: Let us laugh as much as we need, and then we go on with the lesson.

## A student turns round to poke the one behind
LOI: Nam, at the front desk, keeps turning round to poke the pen and tug the shirt of the student behind. The one behind has asked him to stop several times, but Nam laughs and carries on. It looks like fun, yet the student behind is starting to get annoyed.
CHIEU: Five minutes in their shoes | At the end of the lesson, call Nam aside: next lesson I am giving you a special mission. You play the one being poked. You sit at the back, and a volunteer beside you will do exactly what you did to the other student for five minutes, then you both tell me how it felt. Use a different volunteer, not the student who was actually poked, so that student is not put on the spot.
THAY: Nam, I am not blaming you. I want you to try something: sit in the place of the student you were poking and tell me how it feels to be poked like that, again and again.
TRO: Um... probably annoying, sir.
THAY: Annoying how? Describe it for me.
VS: Poking often comes from boredom and a wish for contact, not from malice. Reversing the roles lets the student see the effect for himself instead of hearing a lecture on respect. He draws the lesson himself and so does not resist.
RG: Do not stage anything that pokes him too hard or hurts. Each poke stays light, exactly as strong as what he did. If you see he is poking because of a real conflict with the other student, switch to mediation.
BT: Instead of the role swap, have Nam write three lines: what the student behind may be feeling, and what he will do differently. Then the two shake hands.
CN: I would like you to sit in your classmate's place for five minutes and tell me how it feels.

## A student keeps getting up and walking around
LOI: Hai cannot sit still. Every few minutes he gets up to fetch an eraser, borrow a pen, go to the bin. When told to sit, he says yes, and a moment later he is up again. It is not deliberate mischief; he simply cannot bear to stay seated.
CHIEU: A post with permission to move | Give Hai an official job: Head of Handing Out and Collecting. Twice each lesson he is entitled to stand up for a proper reason: handing out the worksheet at the start and collecting work at the end. Outside those two times, he stays in his seat. Write the post in your record book next to the class monitor's name, so he sees he is trusted.
THAY: I have noticed you need to move more than most people. I am giving you a post: handing out and collecting work. Twice a lesson you may stand freely. The rest of the time you hold your seat for me, all right?
TRO: Yes, sir! I can do that.
VS: Some students need movement in order to concentrate; it is not misbehaviour. Turning that need into an official role keeps him in the class and changes how the class sees him. He moves for a reason, at set times, with trust.
RG: If he cannot sit still in every subject, every day, for months, suggest his parents talk to a specialist about ADHD. Do not add punishment for that behaviour.
BT: Give him a small object to hold while seated, like a squeeze ball or a rubber band, as long as it makes no noise.
CN: I am giving you a job that needs walking around. Can you help me with it?

## A student blurts out answers and cuts in
LOI: Linh always speaks before you have finished the question, interrupts others, and sometimes answers a question you put to a different student. She is truly eager, but classmates are beginning to hesitate to raise their hands.
CHIEU: Three sticks of speech | At the start of the lesson, give Linh three small sticks. Each time she speaks without raising her hand, quietly take one back. A stick left at the end earns a small plus for enthusiasm. If all three are gone, invite her to stay silent for ten minutes and write down what she meant to say, and you will call on her later. She is allowed to speak and she practises patience.
THAY: I really value your enthusiasm. Help me give the others a chance to speak too. You have three sticks; each time you blurt, I take one back. All right?
TRO: Yes. I will try to keep my sticks.
VS: Blurting is a sign of enthusiasm, so it should be shaped, not crushed. What works is a visible system she can track herself, with more praise than penalty. She measures her own progress by the sticks she keeps.
RG: Do not mock her eagerness. Do not tell the class how many sticks she has. This is a private agreement between you and her.
BT: Give her the right to call on another student first, once a lesson, so that yielding the turn is a skill she is practising, not a punishment.
CN: I need you to keep that enthusiasm, and let others speak too. Shall we try it with three sticks?

GN: Good punishments share four traits: short, private, tied to the exact fault, and ending in something useful or a smile.
GN: Ask before you punish: what is this student lacking, tired of, wanting? The behaviour is only the tip.
GN: Never punish a group for one person's fault. Students forgive strictness but not unfairness.
